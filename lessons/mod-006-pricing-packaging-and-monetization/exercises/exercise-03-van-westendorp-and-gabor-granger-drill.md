# Exercise 3 — Van Westendorp and Gabor-Granger drill

**Time:** ~2 hours (plus recruiting / interview time
that runs in the background). **Deliverable:** one
willingness-to-pay research write-up — Van Westendorp
PSM results with the four intersection points, Gabor-
Granger purchase-intent curve, an honest read of what
the small sample can and can't tell you, the price
hypothesis you'd take into Exercise 4.

## Purpose

Run the two willingness-to-pay instruments from
[Lecture 2](../lectures/02-willingness-to-pay-research.md)
against real (or plausible) respondents for the
product from
[Exercise 1](exercise-01-monetization-model-authoring-for-one-product.md)
and the packaging from
[Exercise 2](exercise-02-good-better-best-packaging-drill.md).

The point is not to produce a defensible dollar
figure — the sample is too small for that. The point
is to produce a *directional* read that sharpens the
Exercise 1 pricing hypothesis, plus the honest
methodological write-up that says explicitly what the
sample can and can't tell you.

## Prerequisites

- [Exercise 1](exercise-01-monetization-model-authoring-for-one-product.md)
  complete — the pricing hypothesis is the input
  the WTP research sharpens.
- [Exercise 2](exercise-02-good-better-best-packaging-drill.md)
  complete — you're studying WTP against a specific
  tier (typically the middle / Better tier).
- Access to at least 5–15 respondents in the target
  buyer segment. Real customers or plausible
  prospects, not colleagues. If you don't have
  access, work the exercise against a synthetic
  respondent set — see "Choosing your sample"
  below.

## Choosing your sample

In order of preference:

1. **Real users of the product** who fit the target
   segment. Recruit through the customer-success
   or CS team, or directly from usage data.
2. **Plausible prospects who match the segment.**
   Warm intros, mutual-connection outreach, or
   an existing prospect list.
3. **A synthetic sample** — write 5–10 respondent
   personas from the segment definitions in
   Exercise 1, and role-play the four Van
   Westendorp questions and the Gabor-Granger
   ladder yourself as each persona. This is a
   diagnostic exercise, not a WTP study; the
   results are only defensible as *what the
   personas would say if they answered honestly*,
   not as WTP evidence.

Whichever you pick:

- **Segment the sample.** All respondents in one
  segment produce cleaner curves than mixed. If
  you have two segments, run the study on the
  larger one first.
- **Anchor the product concretely.** Every
  respondent gets the same product description —
  a paragraph, a screenshot, a live demo. Don't
  vary it across respondents; you'll bias the
  curves.
- **Name the reference product for each
  respondent.** The Lecture 2 reference-product
  question — *"if this product didn't exist,
  what would you use instead?"* — is asked first,
  before any pricing question, and the answer is
  captured.

## What the write-up must contain

Three to four pages. Follow the sections below.

### 1. Study design (half page)

- **The product described.** Copy the exact
  paragraph / screenshot / demo you used with
  respondents. If it changed across respondents,
  note the changes (and be aware you've biased
  the curves).
- **The segment.** Named specifically — same
  segment definition as Exercise 1.
- **The sample.** N respondents, how they were
  recruited, whether they had prior product
  experience or not.
- **The reference-product framing.** State that
  you asked the reference-product question up
  front and captured the answer.
- **Van Westendorp question order used.** The
  Lecture 2 recommended order is *too expensive
  → too cheap → expensive → bargain*; note if
  you varied it.
- **Gabor-Granger starting price**s. State that
  you randomized starting prices across
  respondents; list the specific price points
  used.

### 2. Van Westendorp results (one page)

Grounded in
[Lecture 2](../lectures/02-willingness-to-pay-research.md#instrument-1--van-westendorp-price-sensitivity-meter).

- **The four cumulative distribution curves,
  plotted.** Too expensive (share of respondents
  who consider price ≥ P too expensive; increases
  with P). Too cheap (share who consider it too
  cheap at price ≤ P; decreases with P). Expensive
  (share who consider it expensive at ≤ P;
  cumulative from low to high). Bargain (share who
  consider it a bargain at ≥ P; decreases with P).
  A sketch is fine; a real chart is better.
- **The four intersection points.** Point of
  Marginal Cheapness (PMC), Point of Marginal
  Expensiveness (PME), Optimal Price Point (OPP),
  Indifference Price Point (IPP). Read from the
  curves and stated as ranges, not points.
- **The Range of Acceptable Prices**, from PMC
  to PME.
- **Segment cuts.** If you have enough respondents
  to segment (usually only if N ≥ 30), plot the
  curves per segment.

### 3. Gabor-Granger results (three-quarter page)

Grounded in
[Lecture 2](../lectures/02-willingness-to-pay-research.md#instrument-2--gabor-granger-purchase-intent-laddering).

- **The purchase-intent curve.** Price on X-axis,
  share of respondents who would buy at Y-axis.
  Sketch is fine.
- **The knee of the curve.** The price at which
  purchase-intent drops sharply.
- **The revenue-optimal stated-intent price.**
  Price × share-who-would-buy, maximized. State
  the price *and* remind the reader that stated
  intent overstates actual purchase by a
  practitioner-benchmark factor of 2–3×.
- **Segment cuts** where sample allows.

### 4. Reference-product summary (quarter page)

Grounded in
[Lecture 2](../lectures/02-willingness-to-pay-research.md#the-reference-product-method).

- **What respondents named as the reference
  product**, aggregated. Was it a specific
  competitor? An in-house build? A spreadsheet?
  Doing nothing?
- **The reference prices** respondents cited.
- **Anchoring implications.** If most respondents
  named a $500 competitor, their WTP for your
  product is anchored around $500. If most named
  "spreadsheet costing us nothing but it takes 3
  hours a week," their WTP is anchored around the
  labor cost of that time.

### 5. The honest read at your N (half page)

Grounded in
[Lecture 2](../lectures/02-willingness-to-pay-research.md#the-honest-read-at-n--50).

- **What the sample tells you reliably.** The
  Range of Acceptable Prices from Van Westendorp
  is the most defensible output at small N; the
  reference product is the second most.
- **What the sample tells you unreliably.** The
  OPP wobbles a lot at small N — quote it as a
  range, not a point. The absolute purchase-
  intent numbers from Gabor-Granger overstate
  real purchase by ~2–3×.
- **What the sample doesn't tell you.** Segment-
  level WTP if you didn't recruit for segments.
  WTP for buyer roles you didn't sample. WTP for
  competitive scenarios you didn't set up.
- **The specific bias you're most worried about.**
  Category familiarity? Non-buyer respondents?
  Anchoring on your product description? Name it.

### 6. Pricing hypothesis update (quarter page)

- **Updated price ranges from Exercise 1.** How
  the Van Westendorp Range of Acceptable Prices
  and the Gabor-Granger knee sharpen the tier
  price ranges you hypothesized.
- **The experiment you'd run next.** One sentence
  naming the specific price change (or the
  specific price test) you'd take into Exercise
  4 as the experiment brief.

## Starter guidance

- **Run the Van Westendorp first.** It gives you
  the bounds; Gabor-Granger then tests specific
  points inside the bounds. Reversing the order
  produces a Gabor-Granger study anchored on the
  starting prices you picked, not on the
  respondent's real range.
- **Ask the reference-product question before any
  price question.** Once respondents are thinking
  about prices, their reference-product answers
  drift toward whatever they just committed to.
- **In interviews, follow up on every price
  answer.** *"Why that number? What are you
  comparing it to? What would you do if it were
  30% more?"* The follow-ups are where the real
  signal lives; the numbers alone are noise.
- **Segment your respondents deliberately.** A
  mixed-segment sample produces a jumbled curve
  that averages away real signal. Better to have
  N=8 in one segment than N=15 across three.
- **Sketch the curves in real time as respondents
  answer.** You'll notice segment splits fast if
  you plot as you go.
- **Save the raw data.** Every respondent's four
  Van Westendorp answers and every point on
  their Gabor-Granger ladder, in a spreadsheet.
  Future you (or your successor) will thank you
  when you re-run in six months.

## Acceptance criteria

- Study-design section names the product,
  segment, N, recruitment, and question-order
  discipline.
- Van Westendorp results include all four curves
  (sketched or plotted) and the four intersection
  points, stated as ranges.
- Gabor-Granger results include the purchase-
  intent curve and the knee, with a stated
  revenue-optimal price and the disclaimer about
  stated-intent inflation.
- Reference-product section aggregates the
  respondent-named alternatives and their prices.
- Honest-read section explicitly names what the
  sample tells you reliably, unreliably, and
  not at all.
- Pricing hypothesis update sharpens the Exercise
  1 ranges and names the next-step experiment.

## Common failure modes

- **Reporting the OPP as a point.** "The OPP is
  $47." At N < 100 the OPP wobbles across a
  wide range; quote it as a range or a bracket.
- **Reporting stated purchase intent as expected
  conversion.** "60% of respondents would buy at
  $79" is not "60% of new sign-ups will convert
  at $79." Discount by 2–3×.
- **Mixed segments averaged into one curve.**
  Enterprise and self-serve WTP averaged produces
  a curve that describes neither. Segment first;
  average never.
- **Product description varied across
  respondents.** You've moved two variables at
  once; the WTP signal is contaminated by the
  description signal.
- **Reference product not asked.** The single
  most important qualitative output of the study
  is missing.
- **No mention of biases.** The write-up reads as
  if the numbers are precise. Every small-N WTP
  study has known biases; naming them is what
  makes the read honest.
- **Recommending a specific price to ship.** At
  N < 100 the study should inform an
  *experiment* to test the price, not commit to
  a shipped price. Punt the shipping decision to
  Exercise 4.

## Source alignment

The Van Westendorp method comes from Peter H. van
Westendorp, "NSS-Price Sensitivity Meter (PSM) —
A New Approach to Study Consumer Perception of
Prices," 1976 ESOMAR Congress Proceedings —
[ana.esomar.org](https://ana.esomar.org/documents/nss-price-sensitivity-meter-psm-a-new-approach-to-study-consumer-perception-of-prices).
The Gabor-Granger method comes from André Gabor &
C. W. J. Granger, "Price as an Indicator of
Quality: Report on an Enquiry," *Economica*, 33(129),
1966 — [jstor.org/stable/2552031](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2552031).
The honest-read discipline at small N derives from
Patrick Campbell's ProfitWell / Paddle writing —
[paddle.com/blog/author/patrick-campbell](https://www.paddle.com/blog/author/patrick-campbell) —
and Ramanujam & Tacke, *Monetizing Innovation*,
Chapter 5.
