# Quiz — Knowledge check

**Time:** ~20 min. Ten questions. Answer key at the bottom; don't peek.

## Questions

**1.** Steve Blank's central claim about startups vs. established companies
is that a startup is:

- A. A small business optimizing for cash flow.
- B. A temporary organization designed to *search* for a repeatable,
  scalable business model.
- C. A company whose primary distinguishing feature is its use of venture
  capital.
- D. A software team shipping weekly.

**2.** Which of the following is the *value hypothesis* in Eric Ries's
sense (*The Lean Startup*, Chapter 5)?

- A. That a specific channel will produce paid users at a target CAC.
- B. That referral coefficients will exceed 1.0 once the product launches.
- C. That the product delivers value to the customer that the customer
  notices and returns for.
- D. That the burn rate is below the milestone-adjusted plan.

**3.** According to Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test*, the reason "would you
buy this?" is a broken interview question is primarily:

- A. It's illegal in some jurisdictions to ask before disclosing price.
- B. Friendly interviewees will say yes to be polite, producing worthless
  data.
- C. It doesn't include a numeric scale, so results can't be aggregated.
- D. It should be asked at the *start* of the interview, not the end.

**4.** In Christensen et al.'s Jobs-to-be-Done framing (HBR, September 2016),
the correct unit of analysis for a customer purchase is:

- A. The persona.
- B. The demographic segment.
- C. The job the customer is trying to make progress on in a specific
  circumstance.
- D. The pricing tier.

**5.** Sean Ellis's PMF survey asks a single question. Which is it?

- A. "On a scale of 1–10, how likely are you to recommend this product?"
- B. "How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?"
- C. "Would you buy this product again at the current price?"
- D. "What's the one thing you'd change about this product?"

**6.** Ellis's rough empirical threshold for the "very disappointed" response
that suggests pull is present is:

- A. 25%
- B. 40%
- C. 60%
- D. 80%

**7.** A retention curve for a well-fit product typically:

- A. Decays linearly to zero over time.
- B. Rises indefinitely as cohorts age.
- C. Decays and then flattens above zero — a subset of the cohort has made
  the product part of their life.
- D. Fluctuates randomly with no pattern.

**8.** In Teresa Torres's opportunity solution tree, "opportunities" are:

- A. Solutions the team has committed to shipping this quarter.
- B. Customer needs, pains, and desires expressed (ideally) in customer
  language.
- C. Revenue lines forecast for the next fiscal year.
- D. Assumption tests waiting to be scheduled.

**9.** A single interviewee at a niche company loves your product and
uses it three hours a day. The correct response is:

- A. Rebuild the roadmap around that customer.
- B. Ignore them; single data points are noise.
- C. Treat them as a lead — try to find five close analogues to establish
  whether a segment exists there.
- D. Raise a round on the basis of their enthusiasm.

**10.** Which of these is the strongest single indicator that a "PMF"
claim is fragile?

- A. A blended Sean Ellis score, not segmented by cohort or channel.
- B. A retention curve that flattens at a low but non-zero asymptote.
- C. Word-of-mouth referrals from cold users.
- D. A quarter-over-quarter revenue increase in the target segment.

---

## Short-answer prompts

**S1.** In one sentence each, name **two** ways the Sean Ellis 40% survey
can be gamed or produce misleading results, and describe how you'd guard
against each.

**S2.** You have a "very disappointed" score of 45% blended, made up of
70% in Segment A and 10% in Segment B. Explain, in two sentences, why you
should not report the 45% and what you should do instead.

**S3.** Give an example of a discovery hypothesis that is *too vague to
test* and rewrite it into one that a single interview could move your
belief in.

---

## Answer key

<details>
<summary>Click to reveal</summary>

1. **B.** Blank's canonical definition. See *The Startup Owner's Manual*,
   Chapter 1.
2. **C.** Ries defines the *value hypothesis* as whether the product
   actually delivers value the customer notices. The channel/CAC and
   referral answers describe the *growth hypothesis*.
3. **B.** Fitzpatrick's core argument: friendly interviewees give you
   compliments, not signal.
4. **C.** The JTBD framing puts the job-in-circumstance as the unit,
   explicitly displacing persona/demographic framings.
5. **B.** The canonical wording, from Ellis, "The Startup Pyramid," 2009.
6. **B.** 40% is Ellis's often-cited heuristic threshold.
7. **C.** A flattening curve above zero is the honest fit signal;
   decay-to-zero indicates no fit.
8. **B.** In Torres's tree, opportunities are customer-language needs;
   solutions and assumption tests are separate lower layers.
9. **C.** The outlier is a lead, not evidence — see the "outlier"
   anti-pattern in Lecture 4.
10. **A.** All four look positive, but a blended Sean Ellis score hides
   the segment concentration that determines whether "fit" is durable or
   an artifact of one lucky cohort. See Lecture 4, "friendly-network
   false positive" and the Superhuman writeup.

**S1.** Any two of: surveying too warm an audience (guard: exclude the
first N warm users; report cold-only cohorts); changing the wording or
scale (guard: freeze the exact wording and 3-option scale for the life
of the measurement); reporting a blended score across segments (guard:
report per-segment, per-channel, over time); pushing the survey to
users at moments of high satisfaction only (guard: schedule the survey
on a fixed cadence tied to active-user status, not to satisfaction
events).

**S2.** Reporting 45% hides that you have fit inside Segment A and none
in Segment B. You should report the segmented scores separately, name
Segment A as your ICP, and decide whether to concentrate on A or run
discovery to understand why B doesn't retain — not blend them into a
number that guides no decision.

**S3.** *Too vague:* "Users love it." *Testable:* "In the last week,
active users in [target segment] returned to the product on ≥3 days and
completed [key action] at least twice, without a nudge or notification."
Other correct rewrites will (a) name a segment, (b) specify a past-tense
behavior, and (c) attach a frequency or threshold that could be met or
missed.
