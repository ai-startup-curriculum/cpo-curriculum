# Lab — Five-interview discovery sprint + synthesis

**Time:** 6–8 hours across a week (interview scheduling dominates wall time).
**Deliverable:** a discovery-sprint report — a written artifact you'd send
to your co-founders.

## Purpose

Run a real, small discovery cycle end-to-end: hypotheses → recruit →
interview → synthesize → decide. The purpose is not to reach fit; it's to
produce evidence and next-experiment decisions your team can act on.

## Prerequisites

- Exercise A completed for the product you'll interview about — you'll
  test the top three ranked claims.
- Exercise B completed — you have a rewritten script and follow-up
  prompts.
- Five real people in your target segment identified, or a plan to reach
  five within three days. If you're stuck on recruiting: LinkedIn, community
  Slacks/Discords aligned to the segment, alumni networks, and
  cold-outreach with a $50–$100 gift-card honorarium are all reasonable.

## Steps

### Step 1 — Sharpen the hypotheses (30 min)

Take the top three ranked rows from your Exercise A assumption map.
Rewrite each as a specific claim about past behavior that a single
interview could move your belief in. Example:

> Original claim: "Tradespeople reconcile receipts weekly using paper and
> spreadsheets."
> Sharpened: "In the last month, tradespeople in our target segment
> reconciled receipts by re-entering them from a paper folder into a
> spreadsheet or their accounting tool at least once; the process took
> ≥30 minutes and produced at least one error or missing receipt."

Write down what would falsify each claim (e.g., "an interviewee cannot
recall a single reconciliation event in the last month" or "the process
takes < 5 minutes and they never have missing receipts").

### Step 2 — Recruit (Day 1–3)

Aim for five interviews with people in the target segment, using
cold-recruit channels wherever possible. Track your recruiting funnel
(reached out to N, replied M, scheduled K) — recruiting yield is itself
a signal about how hard it will be to acquire customers later.

Recruit tips:

- Offer a short interview (25–30 min) and stick to it.
- Do not describe the product in the outreach; describe the topic ("I'm
  researching how tradespeople handle bookkeeping").
- Offer an honorarium unless the interviewee is clearly seeking
  professional visibility (advisors, community leaders).

### Step 3 — Interview (Day 3–5)

Run five interviews using your rewritten script from Exercise B. Record
with consent; if the interviewee declines recording, take notes by hand
and rewrite them within an hour.

For each interview, capture:

- **Verbatim quotes** for anything specific or emotional.
- **Timeline entries**: a bulleted list of what happened, in order, the
  last time the problem occurred.
- **Named tools and workarounds** with pricing where you can get it.
- **Trigger, frequency, cost**: one line each.
- **Interviewer notes**: which of your interview moves went well or
  poorly. You will drift back toward leading questions; catching it in
  review is how you improve.

### Step 4 — Synthesize into an opportunity solution tree (2–3 hours, Day 5–6)

Cluster interview findings by *opportunity* (a customer need, pain, or
desire in the interviewee's own words). Draw the tree on paper or in a
whiteboard tool of your choice:

- **Root**: the outcome you're trying to achieve (e.g., "tradespeople
  choose our app for weekly reconciliation and retain past month 3").
- **Opportunities**: 3–8 branches, one per clustered need, each labeled
  with the number of interviewees who surfaced it and a representative
  verbatim quote.
- **Solutions**: 1–3 candidate solutions per opportunity, drawn from
  workarounds you saw and product ideas your team already had. Keep
  solutions attached to opportunities — orphan solutions belong on a
  scratch pad, not the tree.
- **Assumption tests**: for the 2–3 solutions you'd invest in next,
  list the assumptions that must hold and the cheapest experiment that
  could falsify each.

The tree is the artifact Torres describes in *Continuous Discovery
Habits*, Chapter 6 —
[producttalk.org](https://www.producttalk.org/continuous-discovery-habits/).
Do not skip drawing it just because you feel you already know the
conclusions; drawing it forces the missing branches to become visible.

### Step 5 — Update the hypotheses (30 min)

Return to your three sharpened hypotheses from Step 1. For each:

- **Supported** — how many of the five interviews produced evidence
  consistent with the claim.
- **Falsified** — how many contradicted it, and how.
- **Uncertain** — how many gave ambiguous or off-topic evidence.

Do not round up. "Two out of five said something adjacent" is not
support; it's a lead.

### Step 6 — Write the report (1 hour)

The discovery-sprint report — one page or so — with these sections:

1. **What we tested**: three sharpened hypotheses and what would
   falsify each.
2. **Who we talked to**: five interviews, one line each — segment fit,
   how recruited, honorarium if any.
3. **What we heard**: 3–5 opportunities with counts and one verbatim
   quote each.
4. **Verdict on each hypothesis**: supported / falsified / uncertain,
   with the count.
5. **Opportunity tree**: attached (screenshot or diagram).
6. **Next experiments**: 2–3 experiments with success and kill
   criteria. Each experiment attaches to a specific solution on the
   tree.
7. **Follow-up commitments**: who owes what by when.

## Success criteria

- Five real interviews, in-segment, no more than one from your personal
  network.
- Each hypothesis has a verdict grounded in interview counts, not
  interpretation.
- The opportunity tree has at least three opportunities, each with a
  verbatim quote and a named interviewee count.
- Each next-experiment card includes both a **success** criterion (what
  would make you invest further) and a **kill** criterion (what would
  make you drop it). Kill criteria are the discipline; success criteria
  without them are wishful thinking.

## Common failure modes

- **Interviewing your existing users.** They already opted in; their
  past behavior with your product is not evidence about the market.
- **Skipping the tree because "we know what we heard."** The tree
  surfaces the orphan solutions and the empty opportunities — do it.
- **Reporting themes without counts.** "Users want offline mode" (n=1)
  and "users want offline mode" (n=5) require different responses.
- **No kill criteria on experiments.** Experiments without kill
  criteria drift into becoming the roadmap by default.
