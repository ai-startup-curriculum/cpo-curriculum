# Exercise 2 — Interview-script critique

**Time:** ~30 min. **Deliverable:** the script below, red-lined; then your
rewritten version.

## Purpose

Build the muscle of spotting leading questions, future-tense speculation,
and pitch-in-disguise moves in an interview script — including your own.

## The script

Below is a plausible-looking discovery script for a receipt-scanning app
aimed at independent tradespeople. It is bad. Read it in full, then work
through the tasks below.

> **Interviewer:** Hi, thanks for taking the time. I'm building an app that
> helps tradespeople like you save time on bookkeeping. I'd love to get your
> reaction. Would that be okay?
>
> 1. So, how do you feel about bookkeeping in general?
> 2. Wouldn't it be great if you could scan a receipt straight from your
>    phone and never touch a folder again?
> 3. On a scale of 1–10, how frustrating is your current bookkeeping setup?
> 4. If we launched an app that did receipt-scanning and mileage tracking
>    for $30/month, would you sign up?
> 5. What features would you want to see in an app like this?
> 6. Do you agree that most tradespeople are underserved by big accounting
>    software?
> 7. If we built this, would you tell your friends about it?
> 8. Any other feedback?

## Tasks

1. **Red-line** each question. For every problem, note which of the
   following it commits (a question can commit more than one):
   - **P** — pitches the product or presumes a positive answer.
   - **F** — asks about future behavior or hypothetical intent.
   - **O** — asks for opinion instead of behavior.
   - **G** — generic ("how do you feel about *X* in general") rather than
     grounded in a specific past instance.
   - **B** — biased scale, or asks for numeric ratings without a behavioral
     anchor.
2. **Rewrite** the script into a version that would satisfy Fitzpatrick's
   three Mom Test rules (past-tense specifics; talk about their life, not
   your idea; talk less, listen more) — see
   [The Mom Test, Chapter 1](https://www.momtestbook.com/).
   Constraint: your rewritten script must be **five questions or fewer**,
   because you're going to spend most of the interview on the follow-ups.
3. **List the follow-up prompts** you'll use to press for specificity after
   each answer (e.g., "and then what did you do?", "when's the last time
   that happened?", "how did you find your current tool?").

## Reference answer for question 4

There is no single right rewrite, but a good version of question 4 might
look like:

> *Walk me through the last time you paid for a piece of software that
> helped with the business side of your work. What was it, what made you
> try it, and are you still using it? If you dropped it, when and why?*

The rewrite works because it asks about a specific past decision, surfaces
the interviewee's actual purchase behavior and threshold, and creates a
follow-up path into churn reasons.

## Success criteria

- Every original question has at least one letter code, and you can
  articulate *which of the three Mom Test rules it breaks* in one sentence.
- Your rewrite contains no questions that a polite interviewee could answer
  with an unqualified "yes" to make you feel good.
- You can defend each surviving question with a specific piece of evidence
  it will surface.

## Common failure modes

- **Rewriting into softer versions of the same question.** "Would you use
  this?" → "Do you think you'd use this?" is not a rewrite; it's a
  euphemism.
- **Losing the follow-up plan.** Questions are cheap; follow-ups are where
  discovery earns its keep. If your rewrite has five perfect questions and
  no follow-up prompts, you have a survey, not an interview.
- **Removing the pitch and replacing it with a bigger pitch at the start.**
  Discovery is not a stealth demo. Say briefly what you're doing and stop.
