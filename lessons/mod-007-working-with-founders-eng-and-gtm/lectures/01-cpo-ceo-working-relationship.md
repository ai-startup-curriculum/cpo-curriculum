# Lecture 1 — Direct partnership with a technical founder-CEO

## The setup

The single most load-bearing relationship in a founding
CPO's job is the one with the CEO — and at the pre-seed
→ Series-B stage the market this curriculum targets, the
CEO is almost always technical and already has product
opinions. That is the *"direct partnership with a
technical CEO"* shape the 2026 postings from Method-
Financial, Clearly-AI, Harper, and Alex-AI ask for by
name. It is not the mature-org shape where a CPO reports
to a CEO who is one to two functions removed from the
product; it is a working partnership in which both people
have hands on the same surface.

Ask a first-time founding CPO what went wrong in a role
that didn't work out, and the answer is almost never
about the discovery craft, the roadmap craft, or the
metrics regime. It is almost always about this
relationship: the CEO kept jumping into the PRD and
rewriting it, the CPO stopped bringing decisions to the
1:1 because "the CEO would just decide anyway," the CEO
promised a feature on a podcast that wasn't on the
roadmap, the CPO escalated a disagreement to the board
before the CEO had heard it, disagreements went silent
until they became firings. Every one of those is a
*structural* failure of the working relationship — an
undefined decision boundary, a missing cadence, an
unstated tie-breaker, a broken escalation path — and
every one is preventable with a small amount of upfront
work.

This lecture teaches that upfront work. Four artifacts,
authored in the first two to four weeks of the role,
that make the rest of the founding-CPO job possible: a
**decision-rights map**, a **weekly cadence**, an
explicit **tie-breaker**, and a **disagreement /
escalation path**.

## The four common failure shapes

Before the artifacts, the failure modes each artifact
prevents. Four shapes recur:

- **CEO-as-PM.** The CEO rewrites the PRD, changes the
  sprint scope midweek, promises a feature externally
  without the roadmap knowing, and treats the CPO as
  an executor. Diagnosis: decision-rights are informal
  and the CEO's default is *"I'll just do it."*
- **CPO-as-executor.** The CPO stops proposing bets, stops
  bringing disagreements, and defaults to "the CEO
  decides." The company is running on one product brain,
  which is exactly what the CPO was hired to prevent.
  Diagnosis: the tie-breaker got read as *"CEO decides
  always"* rather than *"CEO decides after hearing the
  case."*
- **Unmarked overlap.** Both the CEO and the CPO
  independently make decisions on the same surface —
  pricing, hiring, launch scope, GTM messaging — and
  the team gets two answers to every question.
  Diagnosis: no decision-rights map, so every surface
  is contested by default.
- **Silent escalation.** Disagreements never surface in
  the 1:1 because both parties want to keep the peace.
  They surface at the board meeting, in a slack DM to
  an engineer, or as a quiet reason the CPO is
  interviewing elsewhere. Diagnosis: no escalation path,
  so disagreements have no legitimate venue.

Each artifact below prevents one shape structurally.
Getting through eighteen months of a founding-CPO role
without one of these four shapes surfacing is the goal;
the artifacts are how you get there.

## Artifact 1 — The decision-rights map

Bain's RAPID framing (*Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input,
Decide*) — from Paul Rogers and Marcia Blenko's *"Who
Has the D?"* in *HBR* January 2006 —
[hbr.org/2006/01/who-has-the-d](https://hbr.org/2006/01/who-has-the-d-how-clear-decision-roles-enhance-organizational-performance) —
and Bridgespan's DACI (*Driver, Approver, Contributors,
Informed*) are the standard mature-org frameworks for
decision rights. Both are heavier than a founding CPO
needs. The two-person founding-CPO version collapses to
three modes:

1. **Unilateral.** The role decides without asking.
   Named artifacts are informed after the fact.
2. **With input.** The role decides, but only after a
   *specific* input from the other. The other has the
   right to be heard, not the right to veto.
3. **By consensus.** Both roles must agree. If they
   don't agree, the disagreement escalates on the path
   Artifact 4 defines.

A decision-rights map is one row per decision surface,
one column each for **CPO decides how**, **CEO decides
how**. Below is a starting shape; adapt to your
company. The specific *rows* matter more than the
specific *modes*; the row list forces a conversation
about which surfaces exist at all.

| Surface | CPO | CEO |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly roadmap changes within the quarter | Unilateral | Informed |
| Multi-quarter strategy authoring | With input from CPO | Unilateral |
| Feature-level PRD content | Unilateral | With input from CEO |
| Adding a strategic bet to the queue | With input from CPO | Unilateral (bounded by budget) |
| Killing a shipped feature | Unilateral | Informed |
| Pricing / packaging shape | With input from CEO | Consensus |
| Discount / non-standard-terms approval | Informed | Unilateral |
| Hiring the first PM / designer | Unilateral | With input from CEO |
| Firing on the product team | With input from CEO | Consensus |
| Firing on the eng team | Informed | With input from CTO |
| Launch date for a strategic bet | Consensus | Consensus |
| External roadmap communication (podcast, sales deck) | With input from CPO | Unilateral |
| Board narrative — product-progress slice | Unilateral (drafts) | Unilateral (finals) |

Two commitments make this map work:

- **Author it *together*.** A decision-rights map the
  CPO writes alone and hands to the CEO is a proposal
  the CEO will ignore. A map co-authored across two
  1:1s and signed off jointly is a contract.
- **Revise on a cadence.** The map that was right in
  month 1 will be wrong by month 6 as the company
  changes shape. Bake in a semi-annual review; make
  it small enough that reopening the map isn't a
  crisis.

The failure the map prevents is *"unmarked overlap"* —
the case where CPO and CEO independently decide on the
same surface and the team gets two answers.

### Where founders push back on the map

The productive push-back is on specific *rows*, not on
the *concept*. A CEO who resists the whole idea is
signalling that they do not want a real CPO; that is a
signal to take seriously, but it is a role-fit
conversation, not a framework conversation. A CEO who
disagrees on specific rows — *"I want to keep unilateral
on pricing"; "I want consensus on multi-quarter
strategy"* — is engaging with the artifact, and every
one of those is a productive conversation.

Two rows recur as the hardest:

- **Pricing.** Most founder-CEOs at pre-seed / seed
  own pricing by default. The
  [mod-006](../../mod-006-pricing-packaging-and-monetization/README.md)
  ownership-transition happens on this map; expect
  it to be a several-week negotiation.
- **External roadmap communication.** Founders talk
  about the product externally — podcasts, sales
  calls, investor updates. If the CEO stays fully
  unilateral here, expect the "promised on a podcast"
  failure mode. The productive move is *unilateral*
  by the CEO with a *with-input-from-CPO* rider:
  the CEO commits to running any external roadmap
  claim past the CPO before it goes out.

## Artifact 2 — The weekly cadence

A decision-rights map without a cadence is a document
that sits in Notion. The cadence is what makes it live.
The founding-CPO / CEO weekly cadence has three parts;
each has a distinct purpose.

### The weekly 1:1 (45–60 min)

Andrew Grove's discipline from *High Output Management*
Chapter 8 — [penguinrandomhouse.com](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/158477/high-output-management-by-andrew-s-grove/) —
still holds. A 1:1 is *the subordinate's meeting*
(Grove's word; in a partnership shape, adopt "the
individual contributor's meeting"). For the founding
CPO / CEO 1:1, the operational shape:

- **CPO owns the agenda.** The CPO brings a written
  agenda to every 1:1 — three to seven items, in
  priority order. The last standing item is *"anything
  from you?"* for the CEO to raise. Grove's insistence
  on the subordinate-owned agenda applies here even
  though the CPO is not subordinate — the agenda-
  owner is the person with the most operational
  surface, which at pre-seed / seed is the CPO.
- **Decisions get logged.** Every decision made in the
  1:1 gets written down (the CPO owns the note-
  keeping). If it's a decision the CPO made "with
  input" from the CEO, it says so; if it's consensus,
  it says so; if it's the CEO deciding after hearing
  the CPO's case, it says so. This log becomes the
  reference for the decision-rights map review.
- **Escalate disagreements** to Artifact 4 rather
  than defer. The 1:1 is where a disagreement first
  surfaces; if it can't be resolved in the 1:1, it
  goes to the escalation path — it does *not* get
  quietly dropped.

### The weekly product review (60–90 min)

Separate from the 1:1. The product review is where the
CEO sees the current state of the product surface —
what shipped this week, what's in flight, what's next.
The CPO drives; the CEO watches and asks questions.
Two design commitments:

- **Prototype-level fidelity.** The review shows real
  Figma frames or shipped screens, not slides
  describing them. This is where the mod-004
  "own the pixel" discipline pays off.
- **No decisions in the review.** The review is for
  situational awareness. Decisions surface in the
  1:1 or get escalated. If the CEO starts making
  scope calls in the review, the review has drifted
  into the wrong meeting shape.

### The written weekly update (15 min to write, 5 min to read)

A short written note from the CPO to the CEO every
Friday. Not a status report; a *decision-visibility*
document. The three-part shape:

- **Decisions I made this week** — bulleted, one line
  each. The list of unilateral-mode decisions the
  CPO made without needing the CEO. Making the list
  visible defuses the "why didn't I know about that?"
  reaction.
- **Decisions I'm making next week that you should
  know about** — the "with input" ones. Named
  specifically enough that the CEO can raise input
  before the fact.
- **Where I need you** — three or fewer items where
  the CPO needs the CEO's time, decision, or air
  cover. If this section is longer than three items
  most weeks, the decision-rights map has the CPO
  under-empowered; renegotiate.

The written update is the single most under-adopted
artifact in a founding-CPO role. It takes fifteen
minutes on Friday and prevents most of the *"I found
out about that in a slack channel"* failure modes.

## Artifact 3 — The tie-breaker

Every partnership has to answer *"what happens when we
disagree and neither of us changes our mind?"* The
founding-CPO / CEO tie-breaker has two parts and both
must be explicit.

### Part 1 — The CEO has the final call

At founding-team scale, the tie-breaker is that the CEO
decides. The CPO does not have a veto. This is not
a hierarchy statement; it is a *company-shape* statement.
The CEO carries the fiduciary responsibility, is
accountable to the board, and gets to make the last call
on contested decisions. A CPO who insists on veto power
at this stage is misreading the role.

### Part 2 — The CPO has the right to be *heard on the
### specific case* before the decision is final

The CEO's final call is not the same as *"the CEO
decides without hearing you."* The tie-breaker
convention is:

1. Disagreement surfaces in the 1:1 or in a written
   note.
2. Both parties agree it hasn't resolved after one
   focused conversation.
3. The CPO writes a one-page memo: the disagreement in
   one paragraph, the CPO's proposed answer with
   reasoning, the CEO's proposed answer as the CPO
   understands it, the evidence on each side, the
   decision the CPO believes is right.
4. The CEO reads the memo and either agrees, proposes
   a modification, or maintains their position with a
   short written reply.
5. If the CEO maintains their position, the CEO
   decides — but the memo and the reply are logged.

The memo-and-reply discipline forces both parties to
engage with the *specific claim* rather than defaulting
to positional bargaining. It also produces a paper
trail that makes the semi-annual decision-rights map
review honest: patterns of tie-breaker cases against a
single surface are usually a sign the map row is
wrong.

The failure this prevents is *"CPO-as-executor"* — the
case where the CPO stops surfacing disagreements
because "the CEO will just decide anyway." Making the
right-to-be-heard structural, not cultural, keeps that
surface live.

## Artifact 4 — The disagreement / escalation path

The tie-breaker resolves cases where the CEO and the
CPO disagree on a decision one of them owns. It does
*not* resolve cases where one party believes the other
is behaving in a way that damages the working
relationship — the CEO overriding CPO-unilateral
decisions, the CPO going around the CEO to the board,
either party making commitments on the other's surface
without asking. Those need a distinct path.

The founding-team version is simpler than a mature-org
version because the escalation-target list is short. The
recommended path:

- **Level 1 — the 1:1.** Raise it directly in the next
  1:1. Ninety percent of these cases resolve here if
  they're raised early. The failure mode is
  *silence*: the disagreement is real but neither
  party names it.
- **Level 2 — a written memo.** If the 1:1 doesn't
  resolve it, use the same memo shape as the tie-
  breaker. Naming the pattern in writing usually
  produces the acknowledgement that makes it
  resolvable.
- **Level 3 — a trusted third party.** At the
  founding-team scale, this is usually the CTO (if
  there is one) or a board member the CEO trusts and
  the CPO respects. Not a mediator in a formal sense;
  a *witness* who can help both parties hear each
  other. Naming this person *in advance* — during
  the decision-rights map authoring — is important;
  finding them mid-conflict does not work.
- **Level 4 — the resignation conversation.** If
  Levels 1 through 3 all fail, the founding-CPO
  role is not working. This is not a threat; it is
  a genuine acknowledgement that a founding-team
  role requires a working partnership, and if the
  partnership cannot be built, one of the two roles
  will change. The CPO leaves; occasionally the CEO
  does. This is normal and both parties should be
  able to name it without hostility.

The failure this prevents is *silent escalation* —
disagreements that never surface in a legitimate
venue and eventually surface catastrophically.

## Where the four artifacts land inside the mod-004 cadence

The [mod-004](../../mod-004-discovery-delivery-cadence/README.md)
weekly cadence is authored for the *product team*: the
trio, the engineering team, the discovery / delivery
rhythm. The four artifacts in this lecture sit *on top*
of that cadence, not inside it. Concretely:

- **The CPO/CEO 1:1** is one 45–60-minute slot on
  the weekly calendar, separate from any of the
  mod-004 rituals.
- **The weekly product review** is a distinct
  meeting; it sometimes gets folded into the mod-004
  *Friday shipped-slice review* if the CEO is
  attending anyway, but the *purpose* is different
  (situational awareness vs. cross-team review) so
  keep the shapes distinct even if the calendar
  slot is shared.
- **The Friday written update** is a fifteen-minute
  writing task that happens after the mod-004
  Friday review has produced its notes.
- **Tie-breaker memos and escalation memos** are
  ad-hoc; they don't sit on the recurring calendar.

## What the CPO does personally on this seam

- **Authors the decision-rights map**, brings the draft
  to the CEO, negotiates the specific rows.
- **Owns the 1:1 agenda** and the decision log.
- **Writes the Friday update** every week; makes
  visibility structural.
- **Escalates disagreements** to the tie-breaker path
  when the 1:1 doesn't resolve them, rather than
  defaulting to silence.
- **Names the third-party witness** in advance, not
  mid-conflict.
- **Runs the semi-annual decision-rights map review**;
  the CPO owns the artifact even though the CEO
  co-authored it.

## What the CPO consumes from the CEO

- **The multi-quarter strategy** the CEO owns
  (with input from the CPO — see the map). The
  decision-rights map anchors to it.
- **External-commitment visibility.** The CEO tells
  the CPO in advance about anything they're about
  to promise externally (podcast, sales call,
  investor update). This is the CEO's side of the
  external-communication row.
- **Board narrative context.** The CEO tells the
  CPO what the board wants to hear about product
  progress. Chapter 7 turns this into the CPO's
  slice of the board deck.
- **Personnel-change advance notice.** If the CEO
  is planning to fire someone on the eng team,
  the CPO hears about it before the team does.
  Standard partnership discipline.

## Takeaways

- **The CPO / CEO partnership is the load-bearing
  relationship.** Four artifacts, authored in the first
  two to four weeks, make it work: decision-rights
  map, weekly cadence, tie-breaker, escalation path.
- **The decision-rights map** uses three modes
  (unilateral, with input, by consensus) across a
  concrete row list. Author it together; revise semi-
  annually.
- **The weekly cadence** has three parts: 1:1 (CPO
  owns agenda, decisions logged), product review
  (situational awareness, no decisions), Friday
  written update (decisions I made / am making /
  need you on).
- **The tie-breaker** is *"CEO has the final call,
  CPO has the right to be heard on the specific
  case."* The memo-and-reply discipline enforces
  both halves.
- **The escalation path** has four levels: 1:1,
  written memo, trusted third party, resignation
  conversation. Name the third party in advance,
  not mid-conflict.
- **Four failure modes** — CEO-as-PM, CPO-as-
  executor, unmarked overlap, silent escalation —
  each get structurally prevented by one of the
  four artifacts.
