# Lecture 7 — Product progress for the board

## The setup

Every 6–12 weeks — the board-meeting cadence at
pre-seed and seed stage, tighter under active
fundraise conditions — the founding CPO writes
the *product-progress slice* of the board
narrative that the CEO folds into the full board
deck. It is a small, high-leverage artifact: two
to four pages, read by three to seven of the most
influential people in the company's life, with an
outsized effect on how they see the product
trajectory. Getting it wrong at pre-seed is a
missed opportunity to build the board's belief in
the product function; getting it wrong at seed
or Series A can materially damage the next raise.

The founding-CPO failure mode most first-time
CPOs stumble into is thinking the board slice is
either *"a status report"* (it isn't; the board
doesn't want to see the sprint) or *"a fundraise
argument"* (it isn't either; that's the CEO's
job). It is a **product-progress narrative** —
what we learned, what we shipped in service of
that learning, what we're doing next — that
answers three specific questions the board has
about the product function.

This lecture teaches the founding-CPO version of
the artifact and the specific disagreement
discipline for when the fundraise-narrative
needs the CEO would apply to the product story
would distort the product-progress claim. The
deep fundraise-narrative craft — company thesis,
theory of winning, raise-timing, deck structure
— is
[founder-ceo-curriculum](https://github.com/ai-startup-curriculum/founder-ceo-curriculum)
(level 20) and
[startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum](https://github.com/ai-startup-curriculum/startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum)
(level 40). This lecture stays at the CPO slice.

## What the board actually wants from the CPO slice

At founding-team scale, boards don't want the
sprint. They also don't want unearned
optimism. What they want, across three
questions:

1. **Is the product function *learning*?** Not
   "shipping" — learning. What did we test in
   the last cycle, what did we learn, and how
   does that change the bet.
2. **Is the product *building the right thing*
   to win the market position the company
   thesis claims?** Not "did we hit the
   roadmap" — did the roadmap serve the thesis.
3. **Is the CPO trustworthy?** In the specific
   sense of *"do they name what's not working
   as clearly as what is?"* The board makes
   long-horizon judgments about who to back;
   the product-progress slice is a small
   window into who the CPO is under pressure.

Get those three right and the board reads the
slice with rising confidence over quarters. Get
any one of them wrong — no learning, misaligned
with the thesis, spin — and the board's trust
gradually erodes in a way that is expensive to
rebuild.

## The three-part product-progress narrative

The artifact has three sections. Each is roughly
one page. The order matters — learning first,
shipping second, next third — because putting
"shipping" first produces the status-report
failure mode and putting "next" first produces
the fundraise-argument failure mode.

### Section 1 — What we learned (one page)

The load-bearing section. It answers *"is the
product function learning?"* The shape:

- **Two to four specific learnings** from the
  quarter, each written in one paragraph. Not
  "we ran interviews"; the specific *insight*
  the interviews produced.
- **The evidence per learning.** How many
  interviews, what surface of analytics data,
  which experiment or eval. Cite specifically.
  Evidence-free learnings read as
  rationalization.
- **The bet the learning changes.** What we
  believed before, what we now believe, what
  we're doing differently as a result.
- **The learnings that *contradicted* our
  prior belief.** These matter most. A slide
  that shows only learnings that confirmed
  our prior beliefs is a slide the board
  reads as spin.

The failure mode: turning this section into a
list of things the team did. "We ran 30
interviews" is not a learning; "we ran 30
interviews with the target enterprise segment
and found that the ROI story lands cleanly but
the security-review timeline blocks us on
average deals of $50K+" is a learning.

### Section 2 — What we shipped (one page)

The section board members already have partial
context on because the CEO has been updating
them. The CPO's job here is *not* to re-list
the shipping; it is to *connect the ships to
the learnings*.

Shape:

- **Two to four significant ships.** Not
  every ship; the ones the board should know
  about. Grouped by the bet they served.
- **The bet each ship served.** From the
  strategy (mod-003). Every ship should
  connect to a bet the board has seen before;
  if it doesn't, the CPO should name why the
  ship happened outside the strategy (it may
  be a legitimate reason — a security incident,
  a customer commitment — but the connection
  or the deviation needs to be explicit).
- **The outcome the ship produced or is
  producing.** Adoption number, retention
  effect, revenue effect, activation lift.
  Real numbers from the mod-005 analytics
  regime; if the outcome is early and the
  numbers aren't in yet, say so.
- **The ships that didn't produce their
  expected outcome.** Match the "contradicted
  our prior belief" discipline from Section
  1. Not every ship works; the board reads a
  100%-success slide as spin.

### Section 3 — What we're doing next (one page)

The forward-looking section. The board reads it
to answer *"is the CPO's plan for next quarter
coherent with the thesis and honest about what
could go wrong?"*

Shape:

- **The two to four biggest bets for next
  quarter.** From the roadmap (mod-003). Each
  in one paragraph — the bet, the specific
  outcome we're targeting, the discovery /
  delivery approach.
- **The specific risks per bet.** Named.
  "This bet depends on the eval passing at
  the new model tier; if it doesn't, the fall-
  back is X." Risk sections that name only
  known-solvable risks are risk sections the
  board discounts.
- **The one to two things we're *stopping* or
  *deferring*.** The mod-002 kill-list
  discipline applied to the board narrative.
  Naming what the team is *not* doing is a
  strong signal of judgment; every founding
  CPO under-uses this.
- **The one to two asks of the board.** If
  the CPO needs anything from the board —
  intro to a specific customer, feedback on a
  packaging choice, a network expansion — say
  so. Boards want to help; they need to be
  told how.

## The artifact form

Two workable forms:

- **A three-page prose memo.** Best for pre-
  seed and seed boards where the CEO wants
  the full narrative fold-in and the board is
  small (2–4 members). The CPO writes it in
  Google Doc or Notion; the CEO reads it and
  folds relevant chunks into the deck.
- **Six to eight slides.** Best for Series A
  onward where the deck is the primary
  artifact. Each of the three sections gets
  2–3 slides; the CPO owns the slide content
  even if the CEO polishes the design.

Whichever form, three formatting commitments:

- **Real numbers, cited.** Not "engagement
  is up"; "activation on the new onboarding
  went from 42% to 58% in the cohort that
  landed in October." The board reads
  numbers-with-context; the CPO writes the
  context.
- **One graph per section, maximum.** Boards
  read board decks fast; five graphs per
  section is overload. One good graph per
  section — the retention curve, the
  adoption chart, the eval-quality
  trajectory — is enough.
- **No jargon that requires context the
  board doesn't have.** "The MRR compound
  from the top-of-funnel PLG optimization"
  is a sentence the board will pretend to
  understand. Say it in normal English.

## The relationship to the CEO's fundraise narrative

The CEO's board narrative — especially under
active fundraise conditions — has a specific
shape: the company thesis, the market
opportunity, the traction, the ask. The CEO's
material draws on the CPO's product-progress
slice, but selects and shapes it in service of
the fundraise argument.

Most of the time, the two narratives are
coherent — the CEO's fundraise story is well-
served by the honest product-progress narrative
because the fundraise is against genuine
progress. Occasionally, the CEO's fundraise
narrative would benefit from a claim the CPO
knows is not quite true — an activation number
that omits a caveat, a "we shipped X" claim
that omits that X isn't in general availability
yet, a competitive comparison that flatters the
product against a specific competitor.

The founding-CPO discipline for those cases has
two parts, in order.

### Part 1 — Surface the divergence privately, in advance

Before the board meeting. In the CPO / CEO 1:1
(per Lecture 1's cadence). Naming the specific
claim, the specific caveat the fundraise
version omits, and the specific downside if a
board member asks. This is not a confrontation;
it is *co-authoring* the narrative to be both
compelling and defensible.

Most of the time, this resolves it. The CEO
either agrees to add the caveat, agrees to
soften the claim, or explains why the fundraise-
context actually justifies the framing (raise
mechanics sometimes do — the founder-ceo-
curriculum and finance-fundraising-curriculum
are the depth reference). Both parties leave
with the narrative shape both can defend.

### Part 2 — Name what you can't say

If Part 1 doesn't resolve it and the CEO
insists on a claim the CPO believes is
materially misleading, the CPO's move is not
to publicly correct the CEO in the board
meeting. It is to *not endorse* the claim if
asked. Concretely: if a board member turns to
the CPO and says "how do you feel about that
activation number?" the CPO's honest answer
might be "the number is right for the cohort
we measured; the interpretation is stronger
than the sample supports." That is not
throwing the CEO under the bus; it is being
honest under direct question, which the board
notices as trust-worthy.

If the pattern recurs — the CEO regularly
frames things in ways the CPO can't endorse —
the escalation path from
[Lecture 1](01-cpo-ceo-working-relationship.md#artifact-4--the-disagreement--escalation-path)
applies. If it doesn't resolve at Level 3
(trusted third party), it may be a signal that
the working partnership isn't sustainable.

Most founding-CPO / CEO relationships never
reach Part 2. The upfront work in Part 1 —
surface it privately, in advance — is what
keeps them there.

## The board 1:1

At Series A and beyond (sometimes earlier), the
CPO builds a small direct relationship with one
or two board members — usually the lead investor
or a board member who is themselves an ex-CPO or
product-focused operator. The relationship is
*not* an escalation path around the CEO; it is
a *specific* form of relationship that boards
expect executives to have with them.

The 1:1 with a board member has a distinct
shape from the CPO / CEO 1:1:

- **Cadence:** monthly or quarterly, 30–45
  minutes.
- **Purpose:** the board member gets deeper
  context on the product function than the
  board meeting affords; the CPO gets
  seasoned counsel on decisions the CPO is
  weighing.
- **Ground rules:** the CPO tells the CEO
  the 1:1 is happening (transparency); the
  CPO doesn't use it to escalate around the
  CEO (integrity); the CPO doesn't ask the
  board member to make decisions the CEO
  owns (respect for the CEO's role).

The board 1:1 is one of the most under-
adopted founding-CPO practices. Boards
generally welcome it when the ground rules
are clear.

## Common failure modes

Six failure modes recur across founding-CPO
board slices:

- **Status-report shape.** The slide reads
  as a list of things the team did with no
  learning connective tissue. The board
  reads it as low-signal.
- **Fundraise-argument shape.** The slide
  overstates and omits caveats to make the
  raise easier. Short-term wins from this
  are dwarfed by the long-term trust
  damage when the caveats emerge.
- **No named risks.** The forward-looking
  section names bets without naming what
  could go wrong. Boards discount this.
- **No named stops.** The forward-looking
  section names only new starts, never
  stops. Signals over-optimism.
- **Learnings that only confirmed prior
  belief.** The board reads this as
  motivated reasoning. Every quarter has
  contradictory learnings; naming them
  builds trust.
- **Late delivery.** The CPO gets the slice
  to the CEO the night before the board
  meeting, forcing the CEO to fold it in
  under time pressure. The CEO reads this
  as unreliability and starts writing the
  slice themselves, which is where CPO
  ownership of the product story starts to
  compress.

## Deferrals and boundaries

The board slice is a small artifact with a
tight boundary. Several adjacent artifacts are
*not* the CPO's:

- **The company thesis.** The CEO's. If the
  CPO believes the thesis is wrong, that is
  a Lecture 1 escalation, not a board-slice
  rewrite.
- **The fundraise deck as a whole.** The
  CEO's, sometimes co-authored with the
  head of finance. The CPO contributes the
  product slice.
- **The finance model.** The CFO's (or the
  CEO's at pre-seed / seed). The CPO reads
  it to defend pricing (per mod-006) and to
  understand runway (per mod-002); the CPO
  does not author it.
- **The investor updates between board
  meetings.** Also the CEO's, though the
  CPO often provides input on the product
  section.
- **The board decision on raise mechanics.**
  Not the CPO's surface at all.

## What the CPO does personally

- **Drafts the product-progress slice** on
  the board cadence. Two to four pages, three
  sections.
- **Delivers the draft to the CEO** at least
  one week before the board meeting. Not the
  night before.
- **Surfaces divergences with the fundraise
  narrative privately, in advance** — in
  the CPO / CEO 1:1.
- **Builds the board 1:1 relationship**
  transparently, on the shape above.
- **Owns the trust-build over quarters.**
  The board slice is a recurring artifact; a
  single quarter's slide matters less than
  the *pattern* the board sees across four
  or eight of them.

## What the CPO consumes

- **The company thesis and current fundraise
  posture** from the CEO. Both shape what
  the board wants from the slide this
  quarter.
- **The learnings inventory** from the
  discovery cadence (mod-004) and the
  analytics regime (mod-005).
- **The ships inventory** from the delivery
  cadence.
- **The next-quarter bets** from the roadmap
  (mod-003).
- **The prior board slides** (theirs and the
  peer company's if a board member is
  willing to share) — for shape reference.

## Takeaways

- **The board slice answers three questions**:
  is the product function learning, is it
  building for the thesis, is the CPO
  trustworthy under pressure. Not "what did
  we ship."
- **Three sections, in order**: what we
  learned (load-bearing), what we shipped
  (connect to bets), what we're doing next
  (with risks and stops).
- **The learnings that contradicted our
  prior belief matter most**; the ships that
  didn't hit their outcome matter most;
  named stops matter most. Every honest
  admission builds trust.
- **Two-to-four-page memo or six-to-eight
  slides**, one graph per section maximum,
  real numbers cited, no jargon.
- **Surface divergences with the fundraise
  narrative privately, in advance** — in
  the CPO / CEO 1:1. If it doesn't resolve,
  Part 2 is *don't endorse* under direct
  question; escalation path from Lecture 1
  applies for the recurring case.
- **The board 1:1** at Series A and beyond
  is a legitimate, high-value practice with
  three ground rules (transparency with the
  CEO, no escalation-around, no decisions
  outside the CEO's).
- **Six failure modes to design out**:
  status-report shape, fundraise-argument
  shape, no named risks, no named stops,
  only-confirming learnings, late delivery.
