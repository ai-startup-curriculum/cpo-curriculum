# Exercise 4 — Cross-functional influence-without-authority drill

**Time:** ~2 hours. **Deliverable:** one written
pre-alignment memo, one "let me try to convince
you" script applied to a specific disagreement,
one mutual-benefit case translation table, and a
reviewer's memo — against a real (or plausible)
cross-functional misalignment.

## Purpose

Rehearse the four influence moves from
[Lecture 4](../lectures/04-influence-without-authority.md)
— written pre-alignment, "let me try to convince
you," the mutual-benefit case, and counterpart
briefings — against a *specific* cross-functional
disagreement. The exercise's shape is deliberately
tight: one disagreement, four influence artifacts,
one reviewer's memo. The influence craft is a
muscle that only strengthens with concrete reps on
real conflicts, not with theory.

The target failure mode this exercise rehearses
against is the single most common first-time
founding-CPO stumble: *reaching for the CEO* on a
dispute that the four influence moves would have
resolved. Done well, this drill produces a memo
you could actually send on Monday morning.

## Choosing the disagreement

Pick one real cross-functional misalignment where
the CPO's position differs from a specific
counterpart's. In order of preference:

1. **A live disagreement you're carrying today.**
   Highest-value drill. If you have one, use it.
2. **A disagreement from your last role**, or one
   you watched closely on a founding team. Still
   high-value; the reviewer's memo will catch
   whether you're drilling the specific case or a
   generic one.
3. **A plausible synthetic.** If you need to
   fabricate, pick one of the four Lecture 4
   mutual-benefit examples as a starting shape —
   sales committing to an SLA, CS not leading
   with the new packaging, marketing pushing a
   launch date, engineering refusing a debt
   cleanup — and write a one-paragraph setup
   naming the specific company and counterpart.

Whichever you pick, name the counterpart
explicitly (role and name if real, role and
sketch if synthetic). Writing "the head of
sales" without a specific person produces a
generic artifact; "the head of sales — Priya,
ex-Zendesk, hunter-skewed, three-month tenure"
produces a specific one.

## What the artifact bundle must contain

Roughly 4–5 pages. Each artifact is small; the
reps come from completing all four, not from
making any one long.

### 1. The pre-alignment memo (1 page)

The specific written pre-alignment note from
[Lecture 4 §"Written pre-alignment"](../lectures/04-influence-without-authority.md#written-pre-alignment--the-single-most-under-adopted-discipline).
Written in the voice you'd actually send on Slack
or in a shared doc. Shape:

- **The decision you're thinking about** (one
  paragraph). The specific product decision that
  touches the counterpart's surface. Not "we're
  thinking about pricing"; "we're thinking about
  moving the free tier's seat cap from 5 to 3,
  effective April 1."
- **The evidence that drove it** (one
  paragraph). From discovery (mod-001), the
  metrics regime (mod-005), the opportunity queue
  (mod-002), or the AI system map (Exercise 3).
  Specific; cited.
- **The expected outcome** (one paragraph). What
  changes for whom; what you expect to see in
  the data; the window in which you'd call it
  working or not.
- **The specific input you're asking for.** Not
  "any thoughts?"; the two or three questions
  you'd most want the counterpart to answer. "Do
  the deals currently in flight that quote 5
  seats need to be grandfathered? What's the
  smallest cohort you'd pilot this on first?"
- **The reciprocity line.** A sentence noting
  the equivalent surface where you'd expect them
  to pre-align with you. Per Lecture 4 §"Two
  commitments make this stick," reciprocity is
  load-bearing.
- **The SLA.** When you need the input back by,
  and when the decision becomes final if you
  haven't heard back. Not optional; the Lecture
  4 fast-turnaround discipline cuts both ways.

### 2. The "let me try to convince you" script (1 page)

The Lecture 4 §"The 'let me try to convince
you' script" four-step pattern applied to a
*specific* claim where you and the counterpart
disagree. Shape:

- **Step 1 — Name the disagreement.** The
  specific sentence you'd say to open the
  conversation. Include the 15-minute time-box
  and the explicit out — "if I don't convince
  you, we go with your call." Both are load-
  bearing; without them the ask reads as
  lobbying.
- **Step 2 — The case in shared vocabulary.**
  Three to six bullets. The counterpart's own
  outcome-vocabulary (quota, NRR, pipeline,
  velocity, launch narrative). Reach for the
  evidence; cite it in-line.
- **Step 3 — The counterpart's expected
  objections, named.** Two or three. For each,
  the specific piece of evidence or framing
  you'd use to address it, or the honest
  acknowledgement that you can't address it.
- **Step 4 — The out, scripted.** The specific
  sentence you'd say if they don't move: *"OK,
  we go with your call. Here's what I'll commit
  to on my end to make it work."* Lecture 4 is
  explicit: the influence capital comes from
  the *and mean it* half.

### 3. The mutual-benefit translation table (1 page)

A table that translates three to five product-
side asks into counterpart-outcome language. The
Lecture 4 §"The mutual-benefit case" examples are
the shape:

| The ask | Wrong frame (product-side) | Right frame (counterpart outcome) | Counterpart's outcome vocabulary |
|---|---|---|---|
| … | … | … | … |

- **At least one row has to be the specific
  disagreement from Artifacts 1 and 2.** The
  others round out your working vocabulary.
- **"Right frame" lands the ask inside the
  counterpart's measurable outcome** — quota,
  NRR, pipeline-contribution, retention,
  velocity, launch-narrative, incident-rate.
  Not "the roadmap."
- **Flag the row(s) where the mutual-benefit
  case is weak** — where the ask genuinely
  benefits product more than the counterpart,
  and the honest version has to acknowledge
  that. Not every ask lands clean; the honest
  rows build trust more than the clean rows.

### 4. The counterpart briefing design (half page)

The recurring briefing you'd propose with the
counterpart from Lecture 4 §"The counterpart
briefings." For the counterpart you chose:

- **Cadence** (weekly / biweekly / monthly) and
  duration.
- **What you bring** — the two or three recurring
  items from the product side.
- **What you ask for** — the two or three
  recurring items from the counterpart side.
- **The explicit "not a decision meeting"
  framing.** Per Lecture 4 — shared-state, not
  decisions. Decisions surface in the pre-
  alignment memo or in the convince-you
  conversation.
- **The first-meeting agenda.** The specific
  shape of the kickoff.

## Starter guidance

- **Pick a disagreement you can name specifically.**
  "We don't agree on launch cadence" is too
  diffuse; "sales wants to pitch Feature X in
  deals closing before launch and I think they
  should wait" is the right size.
- **Write the pre-alignment memo as if you were
  sending it on Monday.** Not an abstraction;
  the exact Slack / doc you'd paste. The
  specificity is what makes the drill produce
  real behavior change.
- **The convince-you script's time-box is load-
  bearing.** Without "15 minutes" and "if I
  don't convince you, we go with your call,"
  the script reads as "let me lobby you
  indefinitely" and burns influence rather than
  building it.
- **The mutual-benefit case has to be genuine.**
  Lecture 4 is explicit: when the frame isn't
  actually in the counterpart's interest, the
  trust burns fast. Flag the rows where the
  honest version has to acknowledge the ask is
  more for product than for them.
- **Resist the escalation reflex.** The whole
  exercise is a drill in *not* escalating.
  Lecture 4 names escalation-as-influence as
  anti-pattern #1; the reviewer's memo will
  catch whether you quietly fell back to "and
  if that doesn't work I'll go to the CEO."
- **Reciprocity is load-bearing.** The pre-
  alignment memo includes a sentence offering
  the same discipline back. Asymmetric pre-
  alignment fails immediately.

## The reviewer's memo

Half a page, written after the artifact bundle.
Answer:

- **Where does your draft fall into one of the
  six anti-patterns?** From Lecture 4 §"Common
  anti-patterns": escalation-as-influence,
  parallel lobbying, silent RACI overrides,
  authority-borrowing, consensus-seeking,
  withdrawal. Be specific — point at the
  sentence or omission.
- **The counterpart's most likely response.**
  Not the response you'd hope for; the response
  you'd actually get. What's the one piece of
  evidence that would most change your approach
  if the counterpart raised it?
- **The "and if it doesn't work" plan.** If the
  convince-you script doesn't move the
  counterpart and you commit to their call,
  what specifically do you do to help it
  succeed? Lecture 4's "accept the outcome"
  commitment is where most CPOs quietly fall
  off; name what making it stick looks like.
- **Which of the four influence moves do you
  under-use today?** Not in this drill; in your
  working practice. What would it take to make
  it a habit?

## Acceptance criteria

- Pre-alignment memo covers the decision,
  evidence, expected outcome, specific input
  asked for, reciprocity line, and SLA.
- Convince-you script has all four steps
  including the time-box, the shared-vocabulary
  case, named counterpart objections, and the
  explicit out.
- Mutual-benefit translation table has 3–5
  rows, at least one of which is the specific
  Artifact 1-and-2 disagreement, and flags any
  weak-mutual-benefit rows.
- Counterpart briefing design names cadence,
  the recurring items both ways, the "not a
  decision meeting" framing, and a first-
  meeting agenda.
- Every reference to
  [Lecture 4](../lectures/04-influence-without-authority.md)
  cites the specific section rather than the
  lecture as a whole.
- The reviewer's memo answers all four prompts
  and names the specific anti-pattern you most
  risk.

## Common failure modes

- **Escalation disguised as influence.** The
  convince-you script ends with "and I'll bring
  it to the CEO if that doesn't land." That's
  Lecture 4 anti-pattern #1. Fix: Step 4 of
  the script ends at "we go with your call"
  and the escalation lives *only* in a
  distinct Lecture 1 artifact when the four
  moves have genuinely failed.
- **Generic pre-alignment memo.** The memo
  describes a class of decisions, not a
  specific one. Fix: name the SKU, the date,
  the dollar amount, the specific feature.
- **Shared vocabulary isn't actually shared.**
  The convince-you case uses product-side
  jargon (RICE score, PRD under-specification,
  Pareto position) that the counterpart doesn't
  speak. Fix: translate everything into the
  counterpart's own outcome language.
- **Mutual-benefit case is manipulation.** The
  mutual-benefit frame is contorted to pretend
  the ask benefits the counterpart when it
  mostly benefits product. Fix: flag honest-
  rows; sometimes the right move is to make
  the ask on merit and acknowledge it's
  asymmetric.
- **Missing reciprocity.** The pre-alignment
  memo asks for input without offering the
  same discipline back. Fix: a one-sentence
  reciprocity line in every memo.
- **Briefing designed as a decision meeting.**
  The counterpart briefing has agenda items
  that are decisions. Per Lecture 4 — if it's
  a decision, it belongs in a pre-alignment
  memo or a convince-you conversation, not in
  the briefing. Fix: strip decisions out of
  the briefing agenda.
- **No "and mean it" plan.** The reviewer's
  memo doesn't answer "if the counterpart
  wins, what do you do to make it work?"
  Lecture 4's influence capital compounds
  from this part specifically.

Chapters 8–10 —
