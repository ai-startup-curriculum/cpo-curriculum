# Exercise 1 — CPO / CEO decision-rights map

**Time:** ~2 hours. **Deliverable:** one decision-
rights map, one weekly cadence spec, one written
tie-breaker convention, one escalation-path spec,
and a reviewer's memo — for a real (or plausible
synthetic) CPO / CEO partnership.

## Purpose

Author the four artifacts described in
[Lecture 1](../lectures/01-cpo-ceo-working-relationship.md)
that make a founding-CPO / technical-CEO
partnership work: the decision-rights map, the
weekly cadence, the tie-breaker, and the
escalation path. Every subsequent exercise in
this module — and every real founding-CPO role
you take — depends on these artifacts existing
in a shape both parties will actually use.

The goal is not a perfect artifact. It is an
artifact *specific enough to negotiate against*
in your first two-week CPO / CEO alignment
conversation. If the map is too generic to
disagree with, it is too generic to work.

## Choosing the partnership

Use one CPO / CEO partnership you can name
concretely. In order of preference:

1. **The partnership at the company you're
   currently working at (or about to join).**
   The map is real; the negotiation is real;
   the reviewer's memo has stakes.
2. **A partnership you observed closely** —
   your last CPO / CEO seat, or a company where
   you shadowed the founding CPO.
3. **A plausible synthetic** — a pre-seed
   AI-native startup with a technical founder-
   CEO you can describe in a paragraph. If you
   go synthetic, write half a page up front
   naming: the CEO's background (ex-engineer,
   ex-PM, ex-founder), the company stage and
   headcount, the current product surface, and
   your working relationship shape (are you
   the first CPO, replacing a prior head of
   product, joining as a co-founder-level
   hire). The map cannot be judged in the
   absence of them.

Whichever you pick, the artifact is authored *as
if you were the founding CPO today* — not as a
case study of what the company already does.

## What the artifact bundle must contain

Four artifacts. Together, roughly 5–7 pages.
Adopt the shape below.

### 1. Decision-rights map (2 pages)

A table with one row per decision surface,
columns for **CPO decides how** and **CEO
decides how**, using the three modes from
[Lecture 1](../lectures/01-cpo-ceo-working-relationship.md#artifact-1--the-decision-rights-map):
unilateral, with input, by consensus.

- **Adopt or adapt the Lecture 1 starter
  row list** — weekly roadmap changes,
  multi-quarter strategy, PRD content, adding
  a strategic bet, killing a shipped feature,
  pricing / packaging, discount approval,
  hiring PM / designer, firing on product
  team, firing on eng team, launch date for
  strategic bet, external roadmap
  communication, board narrative product
  slice. **Add** at least three rows
  specific to your company (custom-deal
  approval limits, data-partnership scope,
  AI-provider selection, wearable-of-a-third-
  hat by the CPO, etc.).
- **Author both columns.** The map is only
  complete when both CPO and CEO have named
  modes for every row.
- **Flag the rows you expect to be
  contested.** Not annotate the whole map;
  just flag the 2–4 rows where you anticipate
  the CEO will disagree with your proposal.
- **Cite the anchor** for each row's mode
  where non-obvious. "PRD content is
  unilateral because — see Lecture 1 — the
  eng team needs a single voice on this."
- **Name the review cadence.** When does the
  map get reopened? Standard is semi-annual;
  name what event or metric triggers an
  ad-hoc revision.

### 2. Weekly cadence spec (1.5 pages)

Three sub-artifacts as in
[Lecture 1](../lectures/01-cpo-ceo-working-relationship.md#artifact-2--the-weekly-cadence).

- **The weekly 1:1.**
  - Duration and day.
  - Who owns the agenda.
  - The three or four standing items (e.g.,
    open decisions, tie-breaker memos, "from
    the CEO" catch-all).
  - Where the decision log lives.
- **The weekly product review.**
  - Duration, day, and format (in-person /
    remote / async video).
  - Attendees beyond CPO / CEO.
  - The specific artifact-fidelity commitment
    (Figma frames, shipped screens, not
    slides).
  - The explicit "no decisions in the review"
    rule.
- **The Friday written update.**
  - The three-part shape (decisions I made /
    decisions next week / where I need you).
  - Delivery channel (Slack thread, Notion
    doc, email).
  - Length target and turnaround time.

Address the mod-004 cadence overlap
explicitly: which of these slots share
calendar time with mod-004 rituals, and
which are distinct.

### 3. Tie-breaker convention (half page)

Written in the *voice* both parties will use.
Anchor to
[Lecture 1](../lectures/01-cpo-ceo-working-relationship.md#artifact-3--the-tie-breaker):

- The one-sentence statement of the rule
  ("CEO has the final call, CPO has the
  right to be heard on the specific case
  before the decision is final").
- The five-step memo-and-reply pattern.
- Where the memos are logged.
- What triggers the semi-annual review of
  the decision-rights map based on
  tie-breaker patterns.

### 4. Escalation-path spec (half page)

The four levels from
[Lecture 1](../lectures/01-cpo-ceo-working-relationship.md#artifact-4--the-disagreement--escalation-path):

- **Level 1 — the 1:1.** How you'll raise a
  relationship-level disagreement in the
  1:1 (as opposed to a decision-level
  disagreement in the tie-breaker).
- **Level 2 — written memo.** The one-page
  shape.
- **Level 3 — named third party.** Name
  them. If you can't name them today,
  either you don't yet have one (name the
  process for finding one in the first six
  weeks) or the person doesn't exist for
  your company (state so, and describe how
  Level 3 will actually work).
- **Level 4 — the resignation conversation.**
  A sentence acknowledging that a
  partnership that can't recover is a
  legitimate reason for one of the two
  roles to change.

## Starter guidance

- **Author the map *before* you meet with the
  CEO.** A map you bring as a proposal is
  different from a map you build from scratch
  in a meeting. Both parties should have a
  starting position to negotiate against.
- **Expect pricing and external-communication
  rows to be the hardest.** The Lecture 1
  discussion of pricing (which most founder-
  CEOs own by default at pre-seed) and
  external-roadmap-communication (which
  founders talk about externally) predicts
  these will be the multi-week negotiations.
- **Name what a *decision* even is in this
  company.** Some companies decide by written
  proposal, some by meeting, some by "the CEO
  said so on Slack." The map presumes
  decisions are discrete artifacts; if that's
  not currently true, name what has to
  change.
- **Don't over-engineer the cadence.** Three
  small rituals (1:1, review, Friday update)
  are enough for pre-seed / seed. If your
  cadence has seven meetings a week, cut.
- **Name the tie-breaker in *both* voices.**
  A tie-breaker the CPO wrote in their voice
  won't survive the first tough call; a
  jointly-worded one will.

## The reviewer's memo

Half a page, written after the artifact bundle.
Answer:

- **Which row is most likely to get renegotiated
  in the first three months?** Why? What would
  trigger the renegotiation?
- **What will your CEO push back on hardest?**
  Name the specific row(s). What evidence
  would you bring to that pushback conversation?
- **Where does the Friday written update
  break?** The single most under-adopted
  artifact. Under what conditions would you
  stop sending it? What structural counter-
  measure prevents that?
- **If you had to run this bundle for six
  months without amendment, which of the four
  common Lecture 1 failure modes (CEO-as-PM,
  CPO-as-executor, unmarked overlap, silent
  escalation) is most likely to still surface,
  and why?**

## Acceptance criteria

- Decision-rights map has ≥15 rows, both
  columns filled with one of the three modes,
  at least 3 company-specific rows added
  beyond Lecture 1's starter list, and 2–4
  rows flagged as expected-contested.
- Weekly cadence spec names all three sub-
  artifacts with day / duration / attendees /
  outputs / delivery channel.
- Tie-breaker convention includes the one-
  sentence rule and the five-step memo-and-
  reply pattern, in language both parties
  would use.
- Escalation-path spec names all four levels
  and names (or names the process for
  finding) a Level-3 third party specifically.
- Every reference to the Lecture 1 material
  cites the specific section rather than the
  lecture as a whole.
- Reviewer's memo answers all four prompts.

## Common failure modes

- **RACI-shaped map.** The map lists roles
  and responsibilities in RACI (Responsible,
  Accountable, Consulted, Informed) rather
  than in the Lecture 1 three-mode form. RACI
  is heavier than needed at founding-team
  scale; use the three modes.
- **Cadence-as-meetings.** The weekly cadence
  spec has meetings without outputs. Every
  ritual on the cadence has to produce an
  artifact.
- **Tie-breaker-as-hierarchy.** The tie-
  breaker reads as "the CEO decides" without
  the CPO's right-to-be-heard half. This is
  the Lecture 1 CPO-as-executor failure
  mode baked into the artifact.
- **Escalation-path-as-decoration.** The
  Level 3 third party is left as "TBD" or
  "some board member." Name someone or name
  the specific process for naming them.
- **Missing external-communication row.** The
  row that prevents the "promised on a
  podcast" failure mode. If your CEO does
  external speaking, this row is not
  optional.
- **Missing tie-breaker log.** Where do
  tie-breaker memos live? Without a log, the
  semi-annual pattern review Lecture 1
  requires can't happen.

## Source alignment

The three-mode framing derives from the
lightweight founding-team simplification of Bain
& Company's RAPID model — Paul Rogers & Marcia
Blenko, "Who Has the D?" *Harvard Business
Review*, January 2006 —
[hbr.org/2006/01/who-has-the-d](https://hbr.org/2006/01/who-has-the-d-how-clear-decision-roles-enhance-organizational-performance)
— and the Bridgespan DACI framing —
[bridgespan.org](https://www.bridgespan.org/).
The 1:1 discipline derives from Andrew Grove,
*High Output Management* (rev. ed.), Vintage,
1995, Chapter 8 —
[penguinrandomhouse.com](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/158477/high-output-management-by-andrew-s-grove/).
The written-note-as-decision-visibility
discipline derives from Molly Graham, "Give
Away Your Legos," First Round Review, 2019 —
[review.firstround.com/give-away-your-legos-and-other-commandments-for-scaling-startups](https://review.firstround.com/give-away-your-legos-and-other-commandments-for-scaling-startups/).
