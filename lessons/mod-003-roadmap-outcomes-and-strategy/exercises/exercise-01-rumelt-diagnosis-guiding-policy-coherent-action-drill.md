# Exercise 1 — Rumelt diagnosis / guiding-policy / coherent-action drill

**Time:** ~3 hours. **Deliverable:** one product-strategy memo in
Rumelt kernel shape (diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions),
plus a half-page reviewer's memo comparing it to whatever product
strategy currently exists at the same company.

## Purpose

Author a **multi-quarter product strategy** in the shape
[Lecture 1](../lectures/01-rumelt-shape-strategy-for-the-cpo.md)
argues for — Rumelt's three-part kernel, filled with Perri-
shaped outcomes as the content of the guiding policy and the
actions. The strategy is the input every other exercise in this
module depends on, so do not skip to Exercise 2 without a memo
you can defend.

## Choosing the product

Use one product you can name concretely. In order of preference:

1. **The product at the company you're currently working at (or
   about to join).** Highest-value version; the strategy work is
   real.
2. **A product you worked on recently** whose customer base,
   thesis, and current state you remember well enough to author
   as if it were today.
3. **A plausible synthetic** — an AI-native workflow tool for a
   vertical you know, a devtool you'd buy, a marketplace for a
   segment you understand. If you go synthetic, write half a
   page up front naming: the customer segment, the current
   stage (pre-fit, at-fit, scaling), the current-quarter ARR
   band, the company thesis (one sentence), the runway (in
   months). The exercises depend on those inputs; the strategy
   cannot be judged in the absence of them.

Whichever you pick, the strategy is authored *as if you were
the CPO today* — you are not writing a case study; you are
writing an artifact your team would read.

## What the strategy memo must contain

Two to three pages. Adopt the shape from
[Lecture 1](../lectures/01-rumelt-shape-strategy-for-the-cpo.md#the-kernel--three-parts-not-four),
in the following order:

### 1. Company-context header (half page, consumed not authored)

- **Company diagnosis (from the founder-CEO layer).** One
  paragraph. What is the company's Rumelt-shape diagnosis of
  the market and its own position — the theory of winning?
  This is level-20 material owned by
  [founder-ceo-curriculum](https://github.com/ai-startup-curriculum/founder-ceo-curriculum);
  you are *citing* it, not authoring it. If it does not exist
  in written form at your company, note that as your first
  finding — you cannot author the product strategy honestly on
  top of an unwritten company diagnosis.
- **Runway line (consumed).** One line. Current cash-out
  month, per the CFO / founder-CEO. Consumed from level-40
  work owned by
  [startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum](https://github.com/ai-startup-curriculum/startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum);
  do not model it here.

### 2. Product diagnosis (half page)

Rumelt's discipline: a diagnosis *discards information*. In
one paragraph, name the *decisive* product challenge — the
one whose resolution most changes the company's odds of
winning the market position named above. Not a list; the
decisive one.

- **What makes this the decisive challenge, not the
  runners-up?** Cite evidence: discovery findings from
  mod-001, retention / activation data, the customer quotes
  from mod-002's opportunity trees, the loss-reason analysis
  from GTM.
- **What are the runner-up challenges you considered and
  rejected as decisive, and why?** Two or three of them. The
  discipline is in naming what you thought about and *did
  not* elevate; the diagnosis is the discarding.

### 3. Guiding policy (half page)

The overall approach chosen to cope with the diagnosis. A
guiding policy is directional (do X, don't do Y), not
aspirational (be great at X). In two or three sentences:

- **What is the approach?** Concentrate on a segment, invest
  in a surface, defer a class of investment, sequence a
  platform bet — the *shape* of the response to the
  decisive challenge.
- **What is the tie-breaker rule?** For the next allocation
  dispute in this strategy — the sentence the CPO would say
  in the room when two opportunities look plausible. From
  [Lecture 1](../lectures/01-rumelt-shape-strategy-for-the-cpo.md#a-worked-example--bad-and-then-good):
  *"does it shorten the collection loop for a mid-market
  compliance team?"* is a tie-breaker; *"is it high-impact?"*
  is not.

### 4. Coherent actions (half to one page)

Three to five concrete bets. Coherent, in Rumelt's sense —
they reinforce each other, not merely coexist. For each
action:

- **The action.** Named as a product outcome to move (per
  Lecture 2), not as a feature to ship.
- **Why it follows from the guiding policy.** One sentence.
- **How it reinforces the other actions on the list.** One
  sentence — if you cannot say this the coherent-set
  discipline is failing.
- **The falsifier.** A pre-registered metric + horizon that
  would tell you this action was wrong (see
  [mod-002 Lecture 1](../../mod-002-opportunity-assessment-and-prioritization/lectures/01-opportunities-as-bounded-problems.md)
  for the discipline).

### 5. Explicit deferrals (quarter to half page)

Three to five things you are *not* doing this year that a
reasonable stakeholder might ask you to do. For each:

- **The deferral.** The customer request / market opportunity
  / competitor move / internal-request being deferred.
- **Why the guiding policy forces the deferral.** One
  sentence.
- **The move-in signal.** What would tell you the deferral
  should be reconsidered mid-year (per Lecture 2's Not-Now
  discipline).

A strategy without visible deferrals fails the Rumelt
*"good strategy discards"* test.

## Starter guidance

- **Author the diagnosis first, not the actions.** If you start
  with the actions and back-derive the diagnosis, you will
  produce a rationalization of the current backlog. The whole
  point of Rumelt-shape is the diagnosis-first ordering.
- **The diagnosis is one paragraph, not one bullet.** Bullets
  invite the *dog's dinner* shape — a list of things that all
  look important. A paragraph forces you to name the one
  decisive challenge.
- **Steelman the runner-up challenges.** If you can't write a
  good version of *"the decisive challenge is actually
  onboarding, not the collection loop,"* you haven't earned
  the right to reject that alternative.
- **The tie-breaker rule should have a truthy answer for some
  opportunities and a falsy answer for others.** A rule that
  is truthy for everything on your backlog is not a
  tie-breaker; it is a slogan.
- **The falsifier discipline from mod-002 applies here.** Each
  coherent action gets a metric and a horizon; without them
  the action is an aspiration.
- **Deferrals should hurt.** If none of the deferred items has
  a real stakeholder pushing for it, the strategy is
  operating in the space where nothing was at stake anyway.

## The reviewer's memo

Half a page, written after the strategy memo. Answer:

- **Compare to the strategy that currently exists.** What is
  the shape of the current product-strategy document at the
  company (if any)? Which of the four Rumelt bad-strategy
  patterns — fluff, failure to face the challenge, mistaking
  goals for strategy, bad strategic objectives — does the
  current document most resemble, if any?
- **The move that hurt to write.** Which single sentence in
  your strategy memo cost you the most to write — the
  decisive-challenge naming, a specific runner-up rejection,
  the tie-breaker rule, a deferral? What does the difficulty
  tell you?
- **What discovery evidence is thinnest under your
  strategy?** Which of the coherent actions has the weakest
  evidence underneath it, and what's the cheapest discovery
  step (per mod-001) that would strengthen or falsify it?

## Acceptance criteria

- The strategy memo has all five sections in the order above.
- The diagnosis names one decisive challenge, not a list.
- The guiding policy has a named tie-breaker rule.
- Each coherent action has (a) a why-it-follows sentence,
  (b) a why-it-reinforces-the-others sentence, (c) a
  falsifier with metric and horizon.
- At least three deferrals are named, each with a move-in
  signal.
- The company-context header cites the company diagnosis and
  the runway line as *consumed*, not authored.
- The reviewer's memo compares to the existing strategy
  document and answers all three prompts.

## Common failure modes

- **Diagnosis-as-market-size.** *"The market is $47B and
  growing 22%."* That is a market context, not a diagnosis.
  A diagnosis names what is *hard* about winning in that
  market for you specifically.
- **Guiding-policy-as-mission.** *"We will be the best AI-
  native platform for compliance."* Not a policy. A policy is
  directional (X, not Y).
- **Actions-without-falsifiers.** If every action reads *"we
  will invest in X"* with no metric or horizon attached, the
  actions are aspirations, not bets.
- **Deferrals-as-euphemism.** *"We will deprioritize
  enterprise SSO in favor of core capabilities."* Not a
  deferral. A deferral names the specific thing not being
  done, why, and what would move it.
- **Strategy-without-consumption.** A strategy memo that
  never mentions the company thesis or the runway line is
  probably re-deriving one or both, which will get quietly
  re-litigated the first time the CEO gives an investor
  update.

## Source alignment

The kernel shape derives from Richard Rumelt, *Good Strategy
Bad Strategy*, Crown Business, 2011, Chapter 5, "The Kernel
of Good Strategy" —
[crownpublishing.com](https://www.crownpublishing.com/archives/feature/good-strategy-bad-strategy-richard-rumelt).
The outcome-shaped content of the guiding policy and actions
derives from Melissa Perri, *Escaping the Build Trap*,
O'Reilly, 2018 —
[oreilly.com](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/escaping-the-build/9781491973783/).
The strategy-stack layering (company → product → initiatives)
is from Casey Winters, "The Product Strategy Stack," 2019 —
[caseyaccidental.com/the-product-strategy-stack](https://caseyaccidental.com/the-product-strategy-stack/).
