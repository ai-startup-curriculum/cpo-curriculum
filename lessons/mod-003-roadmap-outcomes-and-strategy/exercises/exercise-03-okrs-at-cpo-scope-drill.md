# Exercise 3 — OKRs at CPO scope drill

**Time:** ~2 hours. **Deliverable:** three CPO-scope OKRs
(two to four KRs each) for the upcoming quarter, plus a
half-page scope-boundary memo naming what the OKRs are and
are not for.

## Purpose

Practice authoring **outcome-declaration** OKRs at the CPO
scope — as covered in
[Lecture 3](../lectures/03-okrs-at-cpo-scope.md) — rather
than the Jira-replacement or cascade-to-tasks failure modes
first-time CPOs most often produce. The OKRs are the short
form of the roadmap for the board / peer / team audiences of
Lecture 5.

## What you need before you start

- The strategy memo from
  [Exercise 1](exercise-01-rumelt-diagnosis-guiding-policy-coherent-action-drill.md)
  (the OKRs are how the guiding policy is declared).
- The roadmap from
  [Exercise 2](exercise-02-outcomes-based-roadmap-authoring.md)
  (the KRs express roadmap outcomes as quarterly
  commitments).
- The company OKRs, if they exist. If they don't, note that
  in the scope-boundary memo — you can still author CPO
  OKRs that *ladder to* the strategy, but the missing
  company layer is a finding.

## What the deliverable must contain

### The three OKRs

Two to four objectives, two to four key results per
objective, in the format from
[Lecture 3](../lectures/03-okrs-at-cpo-scope.md#authoring-cpo-scope-krs).
For each objective:

- **Objective.** One qualitative sentence describing what
  the world will look like different at end of quarter if
  this goes well. Not a metric; a direction of change.
- **Key results.** Each KR uses the template:
  *"By [end of Q<N>], we will have moved [named metric]
  from [current value] to [target value] for [named
  segment or cohort], as measured by [named instrument /
  dashboard]."*
- **Committed vs aspirational.** Mark each objective as
  committed (0.9–1.0 expected, missing is a fire drill) or
  aspirational / stretch (0.6–0.7 expected). Do not have
  all three be one flavor.
- **How it ladders.** One sentence per objective linking it
  back to the guiding policy from Exercise 1. If you cannot
  write this sentence, the OKR is untethered.

### The scope-boundary memo

Half a page, written after the OKRs. Answer, in prose:

- **What these OKRs are for.** Who reads them, and what
  decision each reader makes off them (see Lecture 5's
  three-audience frame).
- **What these OKRs are *not* for.** Name at least three
  things people will try to use them for that they should
  not: (a) task tracking, (b) individual performance
  grading, (c) substitute for the strategy, (d) commitment
  to specific ship dates, (e) something specific to your
  company's culture.
- **The cascade contract.** How team-level OKRs will relate
  to these CPO-scope OKRs — team OKRs *ladder to* these,
  they do not *subdivide* these, and teams are allowed to
  author KRs that don't appear here (per Lecture 3). Note
  this explicitly so the cascade discipline is visible from
  day one.
- **The midpoint ritual.** When (week 6 of the quarter) you
  will run confidence checks and what a low-confidence KR
  will trigger.

## Starter guidance

- **Start from the roadmap outcomes, not from the objective
  language.** The KRs should be the *quarterly slice* of the
  roadmap outcomes in Exercise 2. If your KRs don't map
  cleanly back to roadmap outcomes, one of the two
  artifacts is off.
- **A KR without a current value is a wish.** *"Reach 45%
  activation"* is unfinished; *"move activation from 22% to
  45%"* is a KR.
- **Two to four KRs per objective, hard cap.** Any more and
  the objective is doing the work of two — split it, or
  demote a KR to a milestone.
- **Distinguish committed from aspirational visibly.** Board
  readers will treat a KR as committed unless you say
  otherwise; aspirational KRs graded at 0.7 look like
  underperformance if you didn't mark them as stretch.
- **Do not write individual OKRs.** OKRs stop at team
  scope. Individual performance lives in one-on-ones.
- **Ladder-not-cascade.** A team OKR can borrow the CPO's
  objective verbatim, refine the KRs, or author entirely
  different KRs that support the CPO's canopy. All three
  are legal.

## Acceptance criteria

- Two to four objectives, two to four KRs each.
- Every KR has: named metric, current value, target value,
  named segment / cohort, named instrument, dated horizon.
- Every objective is marked committed or aspirational.
- Every objective has a one-sentence link back to the
  guiding policy from Exercise 1.
- The scope-boundary memo names at least three
  what-these-are-*not*-for uses.
- The cascade contract is stated explicitly.
- The midpoint ritual is dated.

## Common failure modes

- **KRs that are milestones.** *"Ship review workflow v1
  by end of Q2"* is a milestone (an input); the KR is the
  outcome the milestone was supposed to move.
- **KRs without instruments.** If you cannot name the
  dashboard / query / survey that measures the KR, you
  cannot honestly score it at end of quarter.
- **All-committed or all-aspirational sheets.** If
  everything is committed, you are not making bets; if
  everything is aspirational, you are not on the hook for
  anything.
- **KRs that are stack rank of the sprint backlog.** If the
  KRs read like the top five items in Jira, you have
  written the backlog twice.
- **Objectives too abstract to grade.** *"Delight
  customers"* is a mood; *"make the collection loop the
  first thing customers point to when comparing us to the
  spreadsheet"* is an objective.

## Common failure modes on the scope-boundary memo

- **The what-these-are-not-for section is missing.** This
  is the section the memo is really about; if you skip it,
  the OKRs will be pulled into task-tracking within a
  quarter.
- **The cascade contract is implicit.** *"Teams will write
  their own"* is not a contract; the contract names
  whether team KRs must subdivide CPO KRs (they must not),
  whether team KRs must exist on the CPO's sheet (they
  need not), and whether individual OKRs are being used
  (they should not be, at this stage).
- **The midpoint ritual is undated.** Undated rituals do
  not happen.

## Source alignment

The OKR mechanic originates in Andrew Grove's *High Output
Management* (Vintage, 1995 —
[penguinrandomhouse.com](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/158477/high-output-management-by-andrew-s-grove/))
and is popularized by John Doerr's *Measure What Matters*
(Portfolio, 2018 —
[whatmatters.com](https://www.whatmatters.com/the-book)).
The 0.7-target norm, the committed / aspirational split,
midpoint confidence checks, and the anti-cascade discipline
are from Christina Wodtke's *Radical Focus* (2nd ed., 2021 —
[eleganthack.com/the-art-of-the-okr](https://eleganthack.com/the-art-of-the-okr/)).
