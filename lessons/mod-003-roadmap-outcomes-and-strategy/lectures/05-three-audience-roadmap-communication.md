# Lecture 5 — Three-audience roadmap communication (board / sideways / team)

## The setup

The founding CPO's single most-consumed deliverable is the
roadmap read-out. It gets read by the board and the founder-CEO,
by the peer heads of GTM / engineering / design, and by the
product-team ICs the CPO leads. The typical first-CPO move is
to author one artifact — usually a slide deck with a Now / Next /
Later table on slide four — and hand the same document to all
three audiences.

That fails predictably. The board reads the slide deck and
misses the *why* underneath the outcomes because it wasn't
written for a reader who doesn't know the current sprint. The
peer heads read it and cannot tell what they're supposed to do
differently. The team reads it and sees a board deck full of
outcome language they can't operationally act on. Two quarters
of this and each audience quietly starts asking for its own
document — which is what the CPO should have authored to begin
with.

This lecture is the discipline: three separately-authored
artifacts, sharing the same underlying strategy and roadmap,
each tuned for what its reader must **do differently** after
reading. The three-audience frame comes out of executive
communication practice, most rigorously argued in Barbara Minto's
*The Pyramid Principle* (Financial Times / Pearson, 3rd ed.,
2010 — [barbaraminto.com](https://www.barbaraminto.com/)), which
distinguishes writing for the reader's *decision* from writing
what the author knows. Camille Fournier's *The Manager's Path*
(O'Reilly, 2017 —
[oreilly.com](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/the-managers-path/9781491973882/))
adapts the same discipline to engineering-leadership
communication in ways that map cleanly to a founding CPO's
three-audience problem.

## The one-artifact failure — why it happens

Two mechanical reasons the single-artifact roadmap gets used
even when it fails:

- **Effort economics.** Authoring three artifacts takes more
  time than one, and the first-CPO is context-switched into
  seventeen roles a day. The math looks like a false economy
  right up until the moment when the board misreads the
  strategy or the team writes a sprint plan against the wrong
  outcome.
- **Consistency anxiety.** *"If I write three documents,
  someone will find the discrepancy between them and it will
  become an argument."* This is a real risk and the answer is
  not to write one document — it is to run a **cross-artifact
  consistency check** (below) that catches discrepancies
  before publication.

The right frame: the strategy (Lecture 1), the roadmap
(Lecture 2), the OKRs (Lecture 3), and the platform bet
(Lecture 4) are the *source of truth*. The three read-outs are
*translations* of that source for readers who have different
decisions to make.

## What each audience is reading for

Before the artifacts, the decision each reader is trying to
make. A read-out that does not answer its reader's decision is
noise no matter how well-authored.

- **Board / founder-CEO.** *"Is the product strategy on-track,
  and if not, what is the CPO doing about it?"* They are
  making a *governance* decision — do I keep confidence in
  this leader / this strategy, do I intervene, do I reshape
  the guiding policy. What they need to see: the diagnosis
  (still true?), the outcomes (moving?), the deferrals (still
  the right ones?), the risks (what could invalidate the
  strategy this quarter?), and — crucially — the *changes*
  since last read-out. They should not need to see the ship
  dates.
- **GTM / eng / design peer heads.** *"What do I need to know
  to align my own function's plan to the product roadmap next
  quarter?"* They are making an *alignment* decision — do I
  hire against this, do I sequence a marketing campaign
  against it, do I plan design research against it, do I
  build the architectural foundation the roadmap needs. What
  they need to see: the outcomes their function needs to
  support, the specific bets that will need their input, the
  deferrals that mean they should stop pursuing certain work,
  and the escalation path if what they see conflicts with
  what they had planned.
- **Product team ICs.** *"How is my current work connected to
  a company outcome, and what should I expect to change
  next?"* They are making a *motivation and calibration*
  decision — do I understand what I'm being asked to move,
  can I trace my work back to something that matters, and
  can I anticipate the reprioritizations that are coming. What
  they need to see: the outcome their team owns, the
  falsifier that would end that work, the discovery evidence
  that generated the outcome in the first place, and the
  interface between their work and adjacent teams'.

Each read-out is optimized for its reader's decision — same
underlying strategy, different framings, different levels of
detail on different things.

## Read-out one — for the board and founder-CEO

**Format.** Written memo, 2–3 pages, or 6–8 slides with speaker
notes. **Not** a slide deck of screenshots from Notion. The
board / CEO read-out is a governance document; treat it as
such. Amazon's six-pager tradition (canonical origin at Amazon
in the mid-2000s, and Bezos's 2016 shareholder letter
[aboutamazon.com](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders)
formalizes the frame publicly) is the shape most founding CPOs
should imitate — prose, tight, and reads without the author in
the room.

**Contents, in order:**

1. **Strategic diagnosis and how it has changed.** One
   paragraph. Whether the diagnosis you authored in Lecture 1
   still holds, and if it has shifted, what shifted and why.
   The board should be able to see whether you're operating
   the strategy you last presented or a modified one.
2. **Outcome scoreboard.** A tight table: each Lecture-3 KR,
   with its current value, target, midpoint confidence, and a
   two-sentence *what we've learned*. Do not present a
   percentage complete for outputs; present the *movement of
   the outcome*.
3. **Bets in flight, and their falsifiers.** For each major
   in-flight bet (the coherent-actions list from Lecture 1),
   the falsifier and whether it has been tripped. This is the
   most-important section for a governance reader: if the
   falsifier has been tripped and the CPO is not shifting
   the guiding policy, that is a signal the board needs.
4. **Deferrals that have re-surfaced.** From the Not-Now
   list on the roadmap (Lecture 2), any items that have
   generated pressure this quarter, what the CPO's answer
   was, and why. This is how the board sees the CPO
   defending the discards.
5. **Risks and what would invalidate the strategy.** The
   two or three most plausible signals that would cause the
   CPO to escalate a strategy revision to the CEO. This is
   the CPO earning the governance-reader's trust: naming
   what would tell you you're wrong before you're forced to
   admit it.
6. **Asks.** What the CPO needs from the board / CEO — a
   hiring approval, a positioning decision, a customer
   introduction, a resolution to a cross-functional dispute.
   Board read-outs without asks train the board to stop
   reading; asks with context train them to keep reading.

**What the board / CEO read-out does not contain:**

- Feature-list rundowns. The board reads outcomes; the
  features live in the delivery plan.
- Sprint / ship-date reports. The date the review workflow
  ships is not a governance-scope fact.
- Team names and internal squad boundaries below the
  outcome layer, unless one is the ask.
- Roadmap-tool screenshots. If a screenshot is the
  clearest way to convey the point, the point wasn't
  clear enough.

## Read-out two — for the GTM / eng / design peers

**Format.** Written memo, 1–2 pages per functional peer, plus
one live walkthrough per quarter. Fournier is explicit that
the manager's job at this seam is to *make the peer function's
own planning easier*, and the artifact is optimized for that
([Fournier, *The Manager's Path*, O'Reilly, 2017, "Managing
Managers" chapter](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/the-managers-path/9781491973882/)).

Because the three peer functions need different things, most
CPOs run this as **one shared narrative memo plus a
function-specific supplement per peer**. The shared narrative
is the roadmap outcomes and the deferrals; the supplement is
what each function specifically needs to plan against.

**Contents of the shared narrative:**

1. **The outcomes we're moving next quarter.** From Lecture
   2's roadmap. Named, targeted, dated on the *outcome*.
2. **The bets we're making to move them.** From Lecture 1's
   coherent-actions list.
3. **The deferrals.** From the Not-Now column of the roadmap.
   This is the section peers most need — GTM stops selling
   what you're not building; eng stops sequencing platform
   work that's been shelved; design stops running research
   for outcomes that aren't in the plan.
4. **The escalation path.** *"If your function's planning
   surfaces a conflict with anything above, escalate here by
   this date."* This is what keeps peers from having to
   invent a workaround silently.

**Contents of the function-specific supplements:**

- **For GTM.** Which segments this quarter's outcomes are
  aimed at; which customer requests the strategy is
  explicitly not going to fund; the messaging the roadmap
  supports (and doesn't); which enterprise pilots are on
  the plan and which are being deferred; the pipeline shape
  the strategy assumes.
- **For engineering.** Which architectural bets underpin the
  quarter's outcomes; which platform / API investments are
  in vs deferred; the sequencing constraints the CPO is
  aware of that could break the plan; the specific
  cross-team dependencies the CPO wants engineering
  leadership to sequence.
- **For design.** Which discovery / research work is in
  flight; the design bets that go with each outcome; the
  discovery evidence the CPO is currently trusting most and
  where the evidence is weakest; the surface-consistency
  questions the platform / point-product bet from Lecture
  4 raises.

**Cadence.** Peer read-outs are typically monthly or
per-planning-cycle. Board / CEO read-outs are typically
quarterly or per-board-meeting. If the peer read-out is going
out only quarterly, alignment will drift between meetings and
you will be doing hallway alignment continuously — which is a
sign the peer read-out cadence is wrong, not that the peer
heads are dropping the ball.

## Read-out three — for the product-team ICs

**Format.** A short written team memo per quarter (a page,
maybe less) plus a live all-hands walkthrough where the CPO
takes questions. The written artifact is what people go back
to when they're deep in a ticket in week seven and can't
remember why the ticket exists.

Marty Cagan's *Empowered* is the best guide for what to write
here. The recurring insight ([Cagan, *Empowered*, Wiley,
2020, Chapters 25–27](https://www.svpg.com/empowered-ordinary-people-extraordinary-products/))
is that product teams get *empowered* by understanding the
*outcome and the reasoning underneath*, and get *disempowered*
by being handed a task list without the outcome. The team
read-out is where that empowerment either happens or does
not.

**Contents:**

1. **The outcomes each team owns this quarter.** From
   Lecture 3's team OKRs, but written for the team members
   themselves — with the discovery evidence, the customer
   quotes, the reasoning underneath. *"Why are we moving
   this outcome?"* is the sentence a team read-out has to
   answer that a peer read-out does not.
2. **The falsifier the CPO is watching.** So the team knows
   what would end their line of work — and so the team can
   raise their hand early if they see the falsifier being
   tripped from the inside.
3. **The deferrals — and why.** So the team knows why they
   are *not* being asked to work on the thing a customer or
   a stakeholder is asking them for.
4. **The interfaces to other teams.** So the team knows
   which adjacent teams' work theirs depends on and vice
   versa; so they can raise blockers early.
5. **What's changed since last read-out.** So the team is
   never surprised by a reprioritization that the CPO
   already knew about.
6. **What the CPO is unsure about.** This is the section
   most first-CPO team read-outs skip; and it is the
   section that most builds team trust when included. The
   discovery evidence is not always complete; some
   outcomes are bets. Admitting the uncertainty is what
   turns the read-out from a memo-from-management into a
   shared operating document.

**What the team read-out does not contain:**

- Board-audience narrative polish. Talk normally; the team
  can tell when they're reading a board deck.
- Sales-narrative framing. GTM's supplement is not the
  team's document.
- Every KR at every level. The team's own KRs plus the
  CPO-scope canopy is enough.

## The cross-artifact consistency check

The single mechanism that keeps three read-outs from
becoming three arguments is a **pre-publication consistency
check**. Before all three read-outs go out, the CPO (or a
trusted deputy) does a fifteen-minute pass with three
questions:

- **The outcome-consistency question.** Do the outcomes
  named in the board read-out, the peer read-out, and the
  team read-out all trace back to the same source-of-truth
  roadmap and the same OKRs? Small differences in framing
  are fine; different targets, different metrics, or
  different segments are not.
- **The deferral-consistency question.** Do the deferrals
  named in each read-out agree? A quiet mismatch here is
  the most common way stakeholders start "discovering"
  that they can lobby the CPO for something the CPO
  already told the team was deferred.
- **The change-log question.** Have all three read-outs
  disclosed the same changes since the previous read-out?
  If the board is being told the strategy is unchanged and
  the team is being told they are re-orienting, the CPO
  is going to get caught inside two quarters.

If the consistency check flags a discrepancy, the fix is
usually in the *source of truth* (the strategy / roadmap /
OKRs) — one of the three read-outs is showing you that the
source of truth wasn't as tight as you thought. Fix it there,
then re-generate.

## The three-read-out anti-pattern to actively avoid

The single most common mistake: **three read-outs, one voice.**
The CPO writes the board read-out first (because that's the
scariest reader), then adapts it for peers by removing some
formalism, then adapts it for the team by removing more
formalism. What comes out is three versions of the same
board-deck-voice document, each of which fails its actual
reader.

The corrective: draft each read-out from the reader's
*decision* backwards. The team read-out is easier to write
if you draft it first (because you're closest to the team),
then the peer read-out (because you know the peer function's
planning cycle), then the board read-out (because now you
know what the team and the peers are seeing and can compress
it for the governance reader). Draft order does not have to
be publication order.

- **Roadmap tooling choices** (Notion, Productboard,
  Aha, ProdPad, Linear) are out of scope for the module.
  Any of them can serve any of the three read-outs; none of
  them save you from having to author the content.

## Takeaways

- Author **three separate read-outs**, sharing the same
  strategy / roadmap / OKRs but tuned for what each reader
  must do differently: **governance** for the board,
  **alignment** for the peer heads, **motivation and
  calibration** for the team.
- Board / CEO read-out is a governance document: diagnosis,
  outcome scoreboard, falsifier status, deferrals, risks,
  asks. No ship dates.
- Peer read-out is one shared narrative plus a
  function-specific supplement per peer. Includes the
  deferrals — peers must know what to stop pursuing.
- Team read-out is empowerment-shaped: outcomes with the
  reasoning, falsifiers, deferrals, changes, and the CPO's
  own uncertainty. Do not talk like a board deck.
- Run a **cross-artifact consistency check** before
  publication — same outcomes, same deferrals, same
  change-log across all three. Any discrepancy is a signal
  the source of truth was not tight enough; fix it there.
